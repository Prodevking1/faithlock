Matthew is the gospel written by someone who assumed you already knew the Hebrew Scriptures. It opens with a genealogy, which is the least inviting first page in the New Testament and a deliberate signal: this story is a continuation, not a beginning. Everything that follows is arranged to make one case, that Jesus is the Messiah Israel had been told to expect, and that the expectation was fulfilled in ways nobody predicted.

It is also the most carefully organized of the four gospels. Once you can see the structure, Matthew becomes much easier to study than its length suggests. This guide covers the background, that structure, seven key passages, and a four-week plan.

## Background: What We Know and What Is Debated

**Date.** Most scholars place Matthew around AD 80 to 90. A minority, including N. T. Wright, argue for a considerably earlier date in the 40s or 50s. The majority dating rests partly on the conclusion that Matthew used Mark as a source, which requires Mark to come first.

**Author.** The gospel is anonymous. It never names its writer, and the tax collector called Matthew appears in it only in the third person. The earliest attribution comes from Papias of Hierapolis, writing in the first half of the second century, who reported that Matthew compiled sayings in the Hebrew language. What Papias meant, and whether his description matches the Greek gospel we have, is disputed. Most scholars today treat the gospel as an anonymous composition by a Jewish author with a strong command of the Hebrew Scriptures and Jewish legal tradition.

**Sources.** The dominant view is the two-source hypothesis: Matthew drew on Mark, sharing around 600 of Mark's verses, and on a hypothetical sayings collection called Q, accounting for roughly 220 verses shared with Luke but absent from Mark, plus material unique to Matthew. A growing number of scholars prefer the Farrer hypothesis, which keeps Matthew's use of Mark but dispenses with Q by having Luke use Matthew directly. Neither is settled, and Q has never been found.

**Audience.** A community of Greek-speaking Jewish Christians, most often located in Syria and frequently in Antioch specifically, though a Galilean setting has also been proposed. The internal evidence for a Jewish-Christian readership is strong: Matthew explains almost nothing about Jewish custom, uses "kingdom of heaven" where Mark and Luke say "kingdom of God" in a way that reflects Jewish reticence about the divine name, and structures his argument around fulfillment of the Hebrew Scriptures.

## The Structure: Five Discourses

This is the key to studying Matthew, and it is not subtle once you know to look. Matthew alternates blocks of narrative with five extended teaching sections, and he marks the end of each discourse with a variation of the same formula: "When Jesus had finished saying these things."

The five discourses:

1. **The Sermon on the Mount (5-7).** Closes at 7:28. The character of kingdom life.
2. **The Mission discourse (10).** Closes at 11:1. Instructions to the Twelve as they are sent out.
3. **The Parables of the Kingdom (13).** Closes at 13:53. Seven parables on how the kingdom grows and who recognizes it.
4. **The Community discourse (18).** Closes at 19:1. Life together: greatness, offense, forgiveness, and confronting a member who sins.
5. **The Olivet discourse (24-25).** Closes at 26:1. The end of the age, and three parables about waiting well.

The pattern has been read by many commentators as an intentional echo of the five books of the Torah, with Jesus as a new lawgiver teaching from a mountain. That reading is widely held rather than universally accepted, but the five-part structure itself is plainly there in the text, marked by Matthew's own formula.

Around the discourses sit the narrative blocks, running from the birth in chapters 1-2 through the passion and resurrection in chapters 26-28.

## The Central Claim

Jesus is the promised Messiah, in continuity with everything Israel had been given, and the kingdom he brings is open to everyone.

Matthew makes the first half of that claim with a device he uses repeatedly: a formula quotation. Over and over he stops the narrative to say that an event happened "to fulfill what the Lord had said through the prophet," and then quotes the Hebrew Scriptures. The first appears in chapter 1:

> "All this took place to fulfill what the Lord had said through the prophet: 'The virgin will conceive and give birth to a son, and they will call him Immanuel' (which means 'God with us')." (Matthew 1:22-23, NIV)

He makes the second half of the claim structurally. The gospel most concerned with Jewish continuity is also the one that puts foreign astrologers at the manger in chapter 2 and ends with a command to make disciples of all nations. Matthew builds that tension in on purpose.

## Seven Key Passages

### Matthew 1:22-23, on the name that frames the book

The quotation above is worth noting for its placement. "God with us" appears at the very start of the gospel, and the last sentence of the gospel is "I am with you always." Matthew opens and closes with the same idea, which is one of the clearer pieces of evidence that this book was designed rather than assembled.

### Matthew 5:14-16, on visibility

> "You are the light of the world. A town built on a hill cannot be hidden. Neither do people light a lamp and put it under a bowl. Instead they put it on its stand, and it gives light to everyone in the house. In the same way, let your light shine before others, that they may see your good deeds and glorify your Father in heaven." (Matthew 5:14-16, NIV)

Two images with different logic. The town on a hill is involuntary: it cannot be hidden, whether it wants to be or not. The lamp is voluntary: someone decides where to put it. Together they say that the visibility is a given and the placement is a choice.

The stated purpose is the last clause, and it does something unexpected. The point of visible good deeds is not that observers think well of you. Matthew, who two chapters later records Jesus warning against practicing righteousness to be seen by others, resolves the tension with direction: the attention is meant to travel through you rather than stop at you.

### Matthew 6:33, on order

> "But seek first his kingdom and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well." (Matthew 6:33, NIV)

The verse sits at the end of a passage on anxiety about food and clothing, which is worth remembering, because it is usually quoted in contexts far more comfortable than the one it addresses. Jesus is talking to people with real material insecurity.

"First" is a sequencing word, not an exclusivity word. The passage does not say the other things do not matter; it says they follow rather than lead. The practical test is not what you value most in the abstract but what gets the first hour, the first decision, and the first claim on your attention.

### Matthew 11:28-30, on the offer

> "Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you and learn from me, for I am gentle and humble in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy and my burden is light." (Matthew 11:28-30, NIV)

The invitation is to rest, and the instrument is a yoke, which is a strange thing to offer a tired person. A yoke is a work harness. Jesus is not offering the absence of load; he is offering a different one, and specifically one designed to fit.

In Jewish usage, "yoke" was a standard metaphor for the obligations of the law and for the teaching of a particular rabbi. The claim in "my yoke" is that Jesus has his own, and the claim in "easy" is a direct comparison with the alternatives on offer, which he criticizes elsewhere as burdens laid on people by teachers who will not lift a finger.

### Matthew 16:15-16, on the question at the center

> "But what about you?" he asked. "Who do you say I am?" Simon Peter answered, "You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God." (Matthew 16:15-16, NIV)

This exchange sits at the structural midpoint of the gospel and functions as a hinge. Before it, Jesus is largely teaching and healing in public. Immediately after it, he begins telling the disciples that he must go to Jerusalem and be killed, and Peter, who has just given the right answer, objects and is rebuked in the strongest terms in the gospel.

The sequence is the lesson. Peter identifies Jesus correctly and immediately reveals that he has the wrong idea of what the title entails. Getting the name right is not the same as understanding the job.

### Matthew 22:37-39, on the summary

> Jesus replied: "'Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind.' This is the first and greatest commandment. And the second is like it: 'Love your neighbor as yourself.'" (Matthew 22:37-39, NIV)

Jesus is answering a question designed as a trap, asked by a legal expert testing him. His answer quotes two passages from the Torah, from Deuteronomy 6 and Leviticus 19, which means he is not producing a new teaching. He is doing an act of ranking.

"The second is like it" is the move that matters. He was asked for one commandment and gave two, joined by a claim that they are of a kind. The gospel that spends the most energy establishing continuity with Jewish law summarizes the whole of it in two lines already inside that law.

### Matthew 28:18-20, on the last paragraph

> Then Jesus came to them and said, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey everything I have commanded you. And surely I am with you always, to the very end of the age." (Matthew 28:18-20, NIV)

Three things in a short paragraph. The claim of universal authority. The commission to all nations, which lands with particular force in the gospel most invested in Israel's story. And the closing promise that answers the name given in chapter 1.

Note that "teaching them to obey everything I have commanded you" points backward at the five discourses. Matthew has spent his whole book assembling the content that this sentence tells the disciples to pass on. The structure and the ending are built for each other.

## The Questions Matthew Puts to a Reader Today

**Who do you say he is, and does your answer include the part Peter got wrong?** Matthew 16 makes clear that the title can be correct and the expectation still be a fantasy.

**What is actually first?** Matthew 6:33 is a sequencing claim, and sequences are visible in a calendar. The abstract answer and the calendar answer often differ.

**Is your yoke his?** Much of what exhausts religious people is not the load Jesus described as light. Matthew 23 is unsparing about teachers who add weight.

**Where does the attention go?** Matthew 5:16 and Matthew 6:1 look contradictory until you notice both are about direction rather than visibility.

## How to Study Matthew in Four Weeks

Twenty-eight chapters, seven a week, one a day. The structure makes this manageable.

**Week 1: Chapters 1-7.** Birth narrative, John the Baptist, temptation, and then the Sermon on the Mount as three chapters read together. Do not break the sermon across a week; read 5-7 in one sitting at least once. Track the fulfillment formula quotations in chapters 1-4 and note which Scriptures Matthew reaches for.

**Week 2: Chapters 8-13.** Miracle sequences, the mission discourse in chapter 10, mounting opposition, and the parables of chapter 13. Chapter 10 is much harsher than most readers expect from a sending-out speech; read it without softening it. In chapter 13, note that Jesus explains why he teaches in parables and that the explanation is uncomfortable.

**Week 3: Chapters 14-20.** Feeding miracles, Peter's confession in chapter 16, the transfiguration, and the community discourse in chapter 18. The section from 16:21 onward is dominated by Jesus predicting his death and the disciples failing to absorb it, three times. Watch the pattern: prediction, misunderstanding, teaching on servanthood.

**Week 4: Chapters 21-28.** Entry into Jerusalem, confrontation, the woes of chapter 23, the Olivet discourse in 24-25, and the passion and resurrection. Chapter 25 contains three parables about waiting, and the last of them, on the sheep and goats, is one of the most demanding passages in the gospels. Finish with 28:16-20.

An exercise worth doing across the whole month: keep a running list of the five discourses and their closing formulas as you hit them. Seeing the frame assemble itself while you read teaches the structure better than reading about it here.

## Discussion Questions for a Group

1. Matthew opens with a genealogy that includes Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Bathsheba, four women with complicated stories. Why do you think they are there in a first-century Jewish genealogy?
2. The gospel most concerned with Jewish continuity begins with foreign astrologers and ends with a mission to all nations. How does Matthew hold those together?
3. Peter gives the right answer in 16:16 and is rebuked six verses later. What does that sequence say about the difference between correct belief and understanding?
4. Jesus offers rest and then hands over a yoke. What load is he describing, and what load is he replacing?
5. Read Matthew 5:16 and 6:1 together. Are they in tension? How do you resolve it?
6. The parable of the sheep and goats in 25:31-46 is one of the most confronting passages in the gospels. What does it say the criterion is, and how do you sit with that?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who wrote the Gospel of Matthew?
The gospel is anonymous. Early tradition, beginning with Papias of Hierapolis in the second century, attributed it to Matthew the tax collector, one of the Twelve. Most modern scholars treat it as an anonymous composition by a Jewish Christian author, partly because the gospel appears to depend on Mark, which is difficult to explain if an eyewitness apostle wrote it.

### What are the five discourses in Matthew?
The Sermon on the Mount (5-7), the Mission discourse (10), the Parables of the Kingdom (13), the Community discourse (18), and the Olivet discourse (24-25). Each ends with a version of the formula "When Jesus had finished saying these things," which is Matthew's own structural marker.

### Why does Matthew say "kingdom of heaven" instead of "kingdom of God"?
Matthew uses "kingdom of heaven" where Mark and Luke generally use "kingdom of God." The most common explanation is that it reflects Jewish reverence about pronouncing the divine name, using "heaven" as a respectful substitute. It is evidence for a Jewish-Christian readership rather than a difference in meaning.

### Is Matthew the first gospel written?
It appears first in the New Testament, and early tradition held that it was written first, which is why it was placed there. Most modern scholars conclude that Mark was written first and that Matthew used it as a source, sharing around 600 of Mark's verses. A minority still argue for Matthean priority.

### How long does it take to read Matthew?
Twenty-eight chapters, roughly two hours read straight through. One chapter a day covers it in four weeks, which is the pace this guide assumes, though the discourse sections read better in whole blocks.
