1 Corinthians is what happens when a church goes wrong in every available direction at once and someone who loves it writes back. Factions, a sexual scandal the congregation was proud of tolerating, members suing each other, chaotic worship, disagreement about food, and finally a group denying the resurrection. Paul addresses all of it in sixteen chapters.

That makes it the most practically useful letter in the New Testament for anyone involved in an actual church, because almost nothing in it is hypothetical. This guide covers the city, the problems, eight key passages, and a four-week plan.

## Background: What We Know and What Is Debated

**Author.** Paul, undisputed, with Sosthenes named alongside him in the opening. One passage, 14:34-35, is widely regarded by textual scholars as a later insertion, and that is worth knowing before you build anything on it.

**Date and place.** Written from Ephesus during Paul's extended stay there, usually dated somewhere in the range AD 53 to 57.

**The city.** Rome destroyed Corinth in 146 BC, and the site lay largely empty for a century until Julius Caesar refounded it as a Roman colony in 44 BC. That history matters: the Corinth Paul knew was barely a hundred years old, populated by freedmen, veterans, and traders from across the empire, with a mixed Roman, Greek, and Jewish population and no entrenched aristocracy. It was a place where status was up for grabs and people competed for it openly.

Its position explains its wealth. Corinth sat on the isthmus connecting the Peloponnese to mainland Greece, with two ports, Lechaeum on the Corinthian Gulf facing Italy and Cenchreae on the Saronic Gulf facing the east. It controlled the traffic between them. It also hosted the Isthmian Games, one of the major athletic festivals of the Greek world, which is worth remembering when Paul reaches for running and boxing imagery in chapter 9.

**One claim to handle carefully.** Many older commentaries and sermons describe Corinth as a byword for sexual immorality, citing Strabo's report that the temple of Aphrodite there employed around a thousand temple prostitutes. This should be treated with caution. Strabo was describing the Greek city destroyed in 146 BC, not the Roman colony Paul wrote to. More significantly, a substantial body of scholarship since the late twentieth century, including work by Stephanie Budin and Julia Assante, has challenged whether sacred prostitution existed as an institution at all, arguing that the ancient reports rest on mistranslation and misunderstanding. Corinth was a busy port city with the moral profile of a busy port city. The thousand-priestess detail is not reliable background.

**How Paul knew what was happening.** Three channels, all named in the letter. He had written to them before, a letter that has not survived, mentioned at 5:9. Members of Chloe's household reported to him about the divisions (1:11). And the Corinthians had sent him a letter with questions, which is why several sections begin with "now about," signalling that he is working through their list.

That means 1 Corinthians is one side of a correspondence. Some of the passages that read strangely are answers to questions we do not have.

## How the Letter Is Laid Out

The organization follows the problems:

- **1-4:** Factions. Members aligning behind Paul, Apollos, Cephas, or Christ.
- **5-6:** A case of sexual immorality, and believers taking each other to court.
- **7:** Marriage, singleness, and divorce, in answer to their question.
- **8-10:** Food sacrificed to idols, and the limits of personal freedom.
- **11-14:** Worship. Head coverings, the Lord's Supper, and spiritual gifts, with chapter 13 in the middle.
- **15:** The resurrection, which Paul treats as the foundation everything else stands on.
- **16:** The collection for Jerusalem, travel plans, and closing greetings.

## Eight Key Passages

### 1 Corinthians 1:18, on the offense

> "For the message of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God." (1 Corinthians 1:18, NIV)

Crucifixion was a Roman method of public humiliation, applied to slaves and rebels and never to citizens. Announcing a crucified man as the world's rightful ruler was not merely implausible to a first-century audience; it was embarrassing, and Paul knows it.

His argument in chapters 1 and 2 is that the embarrassment is structural rather than accidental. A message that could be validated by the standards of Corinthian status competition would have been absorbed by that competition. This one cannot be, which is precisely why the church that was fighting about whose teacher was more impressive needed to hear it first.

### 1 Corinthians 2:2, on the deliberate narrowing

> "For I resolved to know nothing while I was with you except Jesus Christ and him crucified." (1 Corinthians 2:2, NIV)

"Resolved" makes this a decision, not a limitation. Paul was capable of a great deal more, and in a city that prized rhetorical skill he chose not to compete on that ground.

Read alongside 1:18, this is a strategic statement: he refuses to make the message persuasive by the criteria of the audience, because doing so would have transferred the loyalty to the persuader. Chapters 1 through 4 are about exactly that failure mode, and the Corinthians had already fallen into it.

### 1 Corinthians 6:19-20, on the body

> "Do you not know that your bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit, who is in you, whom you have received from God? You are not your own; you were bought at a price. Therefore honor God with your bodies." (1 Corinthians 6:19-20, NIV)

The "you" here is plural in Greek through most of the passage, which is easy to miss in English and changes the emphasis: this is addressed to a community as much as to individuals.

The argument Paul is answering is a slogan the Corinthians apparently used, "I have the right to do anything," which he quotes twice in this chapter before qualifying it. His counterargument is about ownership, not shame. "You are not your own" is a property claim, and the ethics follow from it.

### 1 Corinthians 10:13, on the promise about temptation

> "No temptation has overtaken you except what is common to mankind. And God is faithful; he will not let you be tempted beyond what you can bear. But when you are tempted, he will also provide a way out so that you can endure it." (1 Corinthians 10:13, NIV)

Three claims, and the first is often overlooked. "What is common to mankind" removes the sense of being uniquely afflicted, which is one of the things that makes a private struggle harder to fight.

The third claim is the one people misread. The way out is provided "so that you can endure it," not so that the pressure disappears. Paul is describing an exit that leads through rather than around.

The context is a warning, not a comfort: Paul has just recited a list of Israel's failures in the wilderness and told the Corinthians that anyone who thinks they are standing firm should watch out. Verse 13 is the reassurance attached to a warning, and quoting it alone changes its temperature considerably.

### 1 Corinthians 12:12-13, on the body metaphor

> "Just as a body, though one, has many parts, but all its many parts form one body, so it is with Christ. For we were all baptized by one Spirit so as to form one body—whether Jews or Gentiles, slave or free—and we were all given the one Spirit to drink." (1 Corinthians 12:12-13, NIV)

The body metaphor for a political community was already familiar in Greco-Roman rhetoric, and it was normally used to keep people in their place: the feet should not resent being feet. Paul takes the familiar image and reverses its usual application. The parts that appear weaker are indispensable; the less honorable receive greater honor.

In a church stratified by wealth and status, and competing over which spiritual gifts ranked highest, that reversal is the whole argument.

### 1 Corinthians 13:4-7, on the chapter read at weddings

> "Love is patient, love is kind. It does not envy, it does not boast, it is not proud. It does not dishonor others, it is not self-seeking, it is not easily angered, it keeps no record of wrongs. Love does not delight in evil but rejoices with the truth. It always protects, always trusts, always hopes, always perseveres." (1 Corinthians 13:4-7, NIV)

This chapter was not written about marriage. It sits in the middle of a three-chapter argument about spiritual gifts in a congregation where people were showing off, interrupting each other, and ranking themselves by which gift they had.

Read in that context, every item on the list is a rebuke aimed at something happening in that room. Does not envy: they were envying gifts. Does not boast, is not proud: they were boasting. Is not self-seeking: they were using worship as a display. Keeps no record of wrongs: they were suing each other two chapters earlier.

The passage is more useful, not less, once you know that. A wedding reading makes it aspirational. Its original setting makes it diagnostic.

### 1 Corinthians 15:3-4, on the oldest thing in the letter

> "For what I received I passed on to you as of first importance: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures," (1 Corinthians 15:3-4, NIV)

"What I received I passed on" is technical language for handing on a tradition, and most scholars conclude that Paul is quoting a formula that predates his letter, learned early in his Christian life. If so, this is one of the earliest statements of Christian belief we possess, older than any gospel, and it is embedded in a letter we can date with reasonable confidence to the 50s.

That matters for anyone weighing the historical question. Whatever you conclude about the resurrection, the claim was not a late legend accumulating over generations. It was being recited as a fixed formula within a very short time of the events.

### 1 Corinthians 15:58, on the conclusion Paul draws

> "Therefore, my dear brothers and sisters, stand firm. Let nothing move you. Always give yourselves fully to the work of the Lord, because you know that your labor in the Lord is not in vain." (1 Corinthians 15:58, NIV)

After fifty-seven verses of argument about resurrection bodies, the last word is practical. Paul's conclusion from the resurrection is not contemplation but work.

"Not in vain" is the load-bearing phrase, and it connects back to the start of the chapter, where Paul says that if there is no resurrection, faith is futile. The whole argument is about whether effort in this life carries forward. He concludes that it does, and that this is a reason to keep going on the days it does not feel that way.

## The Questions 1 Corinthians Puts to a Reader Today

**Whose team are you on?** The factions in chapter 1 were formed around genuinely good teachers. Paul's objection is not to their teaching but to the sorting.

**What do you tolerate that you should not, and what do you fight about that does not matter?** Corinth managed both simultaneously, and Paul addresses both in the same letter.

**Does "I have the right to" end your reasoning or start it?** Paul quotes the Corinthian slogan and then spends chapters 8 through 10 explaining why a right you hold is not automatically a right you should use.

**Read 1 Corinthians 13 as a description of your church rather than an aspiration for a marriage. How does it score?**

## How to Study 1 Corinthians in Four Weeks

**Week 1: Chapters 1-6.** Factions, then the scandal, then the lawsuits. Note the pattern in how Paul argues: he almost always starts with a theological claim and derives the behavior from it rather than issuing rules. Watch for the Corinthian slogans he quotes back at them, especially in chapter 6.

**Week 2: Chapters 7-11.** Marriage, food sacrificed to idols, and worship practice. Chapter 7 is more careful and more balanced than its reputation, and Paul repeatedly distinguishes what he has from the Lord from what is his own judgment, which is unusual and worth noticing. Chapters 8-10 are a sustained argument about limiting your own freedom for someone else's sake, and chapter 9 is Paul making himself the example.

**Week 3: Chapters 12-14.** Spiritual gifts, love, and orderly worship. Read all three chapters as one unit, and read chapter 13 last rather than lifting it out. Note the interpretive problem at 14:34-35, which many textual scholars regard as a later insertion and which sits awkwardly with 11:5, where Paul assumes women praying and prophesying in the assembly.

**Week 4: Chapters 15-16.** The resurrection chapter, which is the longest sustained argument in the letter, and then the practical closing. Read chapter 15 in one sitting. Note how Paul structures it: the tradition received, the witnesses listed, the consequences if it is false, then the nature of the resurrection body, then the conclusion in verse 58.

An exercise for the whole month: mark every point where Paul appears to be quoting the Corinthians back to themselves, and every point where he begins a new topic with "now about." The first shows you what they believed; the second shows you their letter's agenda. Between them you can reconstruct a fair amount of the side of the conversation we do not have.

## Discussion Questions for a Group

1. The Corinthian factions formed around good teachers. What does that suggest about how divisions actually start in churches?
2. Paul quotes the slogan "I have the right to do anything" and then qualifies it twice. Where does that slogan appear in your own reasoning?
3. Read 1 Corinthians 13 as a critique of the specific behaviors described in chapters 11, 12, and 14. Which line lands hardest?
4. Paul says the resurrection formula in 15:3-4 was something he received and passed on. What difference does the age of that formula make to how you think about the claim?
5. In chapter 9, Paul argues that he has rights he chooses not to use. What is a right you hold that it might be better not to exercise?
6. 1 Corinthians 10:13 promises a way out "so that you can endure it," not so the pressure ends. Does that match your experience?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When and where was 1 Corinthians written?
From Ephesus, during Paul's extended stay there, usually dated in the range AD 53 to 57. It is not the first letter he sent to Corinth; he refers at 5:9 to an earlier one that has not survived.

### What problems does 1 Corinthians address?
Factions (1-4), a case of sexual immorality and lawsuits between believers (5-6), questions about marriage and singleness (7), food sacrificed to idols and the use of personal freedom (8-10), disorder in worship including head coverings, the Lord's Supper and spiritual gifts (11-14), and a denial of the resurrection (15).

### Is 1 Corinthians 13 about marriage?
No. It sits in the middle of Paul's argument about spiritual gifts in a divided congregation, and each item in the list corresponds to something going wrong in that church. It works beautifully at weddings, but reading it in its original setting turns it from an ideal into a diagnosis.

### Was Corinth really as immoral as people say?
It was a wealthy, transient port city, and Paul clearly addresses real sexual problems in the congregation. The specific claim that the temple of Aphrodite employed a thousand temple prostitutes comes from Strabo and describes the Greek city destroyed in 146 BC, not the Roman colony of Paul's day. Beyond that, whether institutional sacred prostitution existed at all is now seriously disputed by scholars. The detail is repeated often and is not solid ground.

### How long does it take to read 1 Corinthians?
Sixteen chapters, roughly an hour and ten minutes read straight through. Chapters 13 and 15 are the ones most worth reading repeatedly.
