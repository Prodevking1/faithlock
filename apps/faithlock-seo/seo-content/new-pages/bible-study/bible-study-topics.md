Most people looking for Bible study topics are not short of options. They are stuck between two bad ones: open at Genesis and lose momentum somewhere in Leviticus, or pick a topic at random and end up with a folder of verses that never connected to anything.

There are two honest ways to organize a study, and they answer different questions. **Book studies** ask what one author said to one audience, and they protect you from the most common error in topical work, which is collecting verses that agree with you. **Topical studies** ask what the whole Bible says about one thing, and they are how Scripture actually meets a Tuesday.

This page indexes both. Each entry says what is genuinely in it, so you can choose on substance rather than on which title sounds most like your current mood.

Scripture quotations here are from the New International Version.

---

## How to Choose, in Three Questions

**Are you studying alone or with a group?** Groups do better with books, because everyone is on the same page and nobody has to have prepared. Individuals do better with topics when there is a live question and better with books when there is not.

**How much time per sitting?** Twenty minutes suits a topical study or a short epistle. An hour suits Genesis or John. Be honest about this before you pick, because the single strongest predictor of finishing is whether the study fits the time you actually have.

**Is there a live question?** If something specific is going on, money, a diagnosis, a broken relationship, go topical and go directly at it. If nothing in particular is going on, go by book. Books are how you build the furniture you will need later, when there is no time to build it.

## Studies by Book

### New Testament

**[Matthew](/resources/bible-study-on-matthew)** presents Jesus as the fulfillment of Israel's story and organizes his teaching into five large discourses, of which the Sermon on the Mount is the first. It is the best gospel to study when you want the ethical demands stated plainly and at length, and it is the one most concerned with what a community of disciples is supposed to look like.

**[John](/resources/bible-study-on-john)** is structurally unlike the other three gospels: fewer events, much longer discourses, seven signs, and the "I am" sayings. Study it when you want the question of who Jesus claims to be put directly rather than developed by implication.

**[Romans](/resources/bible-study-on-romans)** is Paul's most systematic letter and the closest thing the New Testament has to a sustained argument, running from human guilt through justification and the life of the Spirit to Israel's place and then to daily conduct. It is demanding, it rewards slow reading, and it is the letter behind more theological arguments than any other.

**[1 Corinthians](/resources/bible-study-on-1-corinthians)** is the opposite: a pastor answering a difficult church's actual questions about division, lawsuits, sex, food, worship, spiritual gifts and resurrection. Study it when you want to see doctrine applied to a mess rather than developed in the abstract, and note that chapter 13 belongs to an argument about gifts rather than to weddings.

**[Galatians](/resources/bible-study-on-galatians)** is short, furious, and about one thing: whether Gentile believers must take on the law. It contains Paul's account of his confrontation with Peter and the clearest statement of Christian freedom in the New Testament, which is why it functions as a compressed Romans.

**[Ephesians](/resources/bible-study-on-ephesians)** splits cleanly at chapter 4: three chapters on what God has done, three on how to live in light of it. The first half contains the letter's famous sentences on grace and on the dividing wall, and the second half moves through speech, work, marriage, households and the armor of God.

**[Philippians](/resources/bible-study-on-philippians)** is a thank-you letter written from prison that keeps returning to joy, which is either strange or the entire point. It contains the Christ hymn of chapter 2 and the contentment passage of chapter 4, and it is short enough to reread whole every week for a month.

**[James](/resources/bible-study-on-james)** reads like wisdom literature rather than argument: roughly fifty commands in five chapters, on money, speech, favoritism, patience, and the gap between claimed faith and visible action. It is the most practical book in the New Testament and the one Luther liked least.

### Old Testament

**[Genesis](/resources/bible-study-on-genesis)** sets up everything that follows: creation, the fall, the flood, Babel, and then the long family narrative of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph. Study it when you want to understand why the rest of the Bible assumes what it assumes, and expect the interpretive questions about the opening chapters to require some patience.

**[Psalms](/resources/bible-study-on-psalms)** is the Bible's prayer book and its most honest one, containing praise, thanksgiving, wisdom, and a large body of lament that gives permission most Christians did not know they had. It is the best book to study in a hard season, and it is the only one designed to be prayed rather than analyzed.

**[Proverbs](/resources/bible-study-on-proverbs)** is practical wisdom on speech, work, money, friendship, appetite and folly, written as generally reliable observations rather than promises. Reading it as a set of guarantees is the standard mistake and produces a lot of unnecessary disappointment.

## Studies by Theme

These are shorter, usually one sitting each, built around a single question and anchored in one passage.

### Self-control and habit

- **[Proverbs on self-control](/resources/proverbs-on-self-control)** collects the wisdom sayings on appetite and restraint, including the city with broken walls.
- **[James on self-control](/resources/james-on-self-control)** works through the tongue passages: the bit, the rudder, the spark in the forest.
- **[Romans on temptation](/resources/romans-on-temptation)** deals with Paul's account in chapter 7 of doing the thing he does not want to do, which is the passage most people need and few have read in context.
- **[Daniel on discipline](/resources/daniel-on-discipline)** looks at a young man's decision about food and a much older man's decision about a window, and what they have in common.
- **[Bible verses about discipline](/resources/bible-verses-about-discipline)** and **[bible verses about self-control](/resources/bible-verses-about-self-control)** are the verse collections behind these.

### Time, focus and rest

- **[Ecclesiastes on time](/resources/ecclesiastes-on-time)** is the hardest and most useful of these, since it refuses to make your days mean more than they do and somehow leaves you freer.
- **[Ephesians on time](/resources/ephesians-on-time)** takes up "making the most of every opportunity" and what Paul actually meant by it.
- **[Colossians on focus](/resources/colossians-on-focus)** works from the instruction to set your mind on things above, which is a claim about attention rather than about mood.
- **[Jesus on rest](/resources/jesus-on-rest)** and **[jesus on priorities](/resources/jesus-on-priorities)** cover the invitation of Matthew 11 and the ordering questions of the Sermon on the Mount.
- **[Bible verses about rest](/resources/bible-verses-about-rest)** collects the Sabbath texts.

### Worry, fear and anxiety

- **[Jesus on worry](/resources/jesus-on-worry)** is Matthew 6 read closely, including the observation that the passage argues rather than merely reassures.
- **[Bible verses about anxiety](/resources/bible-verses-about-anxiety)**, **[about fear](/resources/bible-verses-about-fear)** and **[about worry](/resources/bible-verses-about-worry)** are the topical collections, and Philippians 4 sits behind most of them.

### Money, work and contentment

- **[Philippians on contentment](/resources/philippians-on-contentment)** takes the "secret" Paul says he learned and asks what learning it involved.
- **[Proverbs on laziness](/resources/proverbs-on-laziness)** is bracing and short.
- **[Bible verses about money](/resources/bible-verses-about-money)**, **[about work](/resources/bible-verses-about-work)** and **[about generosity](/resources/bible-verses-about-generosity)** cover the wider material.

### Speech and relationships

- **[Proverbs on speech](/resources/proverbs-on-speech)** is the densest chapter-by-chapter material in the Bible on what to say and when not to.
- **[Bible verses about forgiveness](/resources/bible-verses-about-forgiveness)**, **[about anger](/resources/bible-verses-about-anger)** and **[about relationships](/resources/bible-verses-about-relationships)** are the topical entries.

### Freedom, grace and identity

- **[Galatians on freedom](/resources/galatians-on-freedom)** is the anchor study, and it is more specific than the word "freedom" usually suggests.
- **[Bible verses about grace](/resources/bible-verses-about-grace)**, **[about identity](/resources/bible-verses-about-identity)** and **[about shame](/resources/bible-verses-about-shame)** follow from it.

### Suffering, grief and waiting

- **[Bible verses about suffering](/resources/bible-verses-about-suffering)**, **[about grief](/resources/bible-verses-about-grief)**, **[about waiting on god](/resources/bible-verses-about-waiting-on-god)** and **[about perseverance](/resources/bible-verses-about-perseverance)** are the collections. Pair any of them with the [Psalms study](/resources/bible-study-on-psalms), because the laments do something a verse list cannot.

## Topics With No Page Here Yet

Some of the best studies are ones you build. A workable method: pick the question, find the two or three passages that treat it at length rather than in passing, read those in full context, and only then go looking for supporting verses.

Worth building this way:

**The parables**, taken as a set rather than one at a time. Matthew 13, Luke 15 and Luke 16 together will tell you more about how Jesus taught than any single famous parable does.

**The Sermon on the Mount**, Matthew 5 to 7, as a four-week study. It is three chapters and it will take a month, which tells you something.

**The prophets on justice**, especially Amos, Micah 6 and Isaiah 58. Uncomfortable, and the passages are usually quoted in fragments.

**Ruth or Esther**, either of which is a complete narrative study in a single sitting and both of which are frequently underrated as group material.

**Acts**, read as the sequel to Luke, which is how it was written and rarely how it is taught.

**Hebrews**, if your group has stamina. It is the most sustained argument in the New Testament after Romans and the one most dependent on knowing the Old Testament first.

## What the Bible Says About Studying It

Two verses worth having in mind before you start.

> "All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting and training in righteousness, so that the servant of God may be thoroughly equipped for every good work." (2 Timothy 3:16-17, NIV)

Note the four verbs. Teaching and training are the comfortable two; rebuking and correcting are in the same sentence and carry the same weight. A study that only ever confirms you is not doing two of the four things the text says Scripture is for. This is the strongest argument for book studies over purely topical ones, since a book will raise subjects you did not select.

> "Now the Berean Jews were of more noble character than those in Thessalonica, for they received the message with great eagerness and examined the Scriptures every day to see if what Paul said was true." (Acts 17:11, NIV)

Eagerness and examination in the same sentence, and the examination is aimed at an apostle. Checking is presented as commendable rather than as a failure of trust.

## How Long Each Study Actually Takes

Estimates for one pass at a reasonable pace, reading and thinking rather than skimming.

| Study | Sittings | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| A single topical study | 1 | Twenty to forty minutes |
| Philippians, Galatians, James | 4 | One chapter group per week |
| Ephesians, 1 Corinthians | 6 to 8 | 1 Corinthians is longer than it looks |
| Matthew, John | 10 to 12 | Longer if you take the discourses slowly |
| Romans | 12 to 16 | The hardest on this list |
| Genesis | 12 | Chapters 1 to 11 will take a third of it |
| Psalms, Proverbs | Open-ended | Better as a long habit than a project |

The honest note about all of these: the number of sittings is rarely what stops people. Assembling one uninterrupted forty-minute block is.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are good Bible study topics for beginners?
Start with a short book rather than a topic. Philippians, James or Mark are the usual three, because each is finishable in about four weeks and none requires background reading. If you want a topical entry point instead, [jesus on worry](/resources/jesus-on-worry) or [proverbs on self-control](/resources/proverbs-on-self-control) are self-contained single sittings.

### What is the difference between a topical and a book study?
A book study follows one author's argument to one audience from start to finish, so it raises subjects you would not have chosen. A topical study gathers what the whole Bible says about one theme, which is more immediately useful and more prone to selection bias. Most people benefit from alternating: a book, then a topic, then another book.

### What are good Bible study topics for a small group?
Books work better than topics in groups, because everyone arrives at the same passage and nobody needs to have prepared. James, Philippians and Ephesians are reliable for mixed groups. Avoid Romans and Revelation for a group's first study, since both require sustained commitment before they pay off.

### How do I choose a Bible study topic?
Ask whether there is a live question in your life right now. If there is, go topical and go directly at it. If there is not, take a book, because that is when you build understanding you will need later, when there is no time to build it.

### How long should a Bible study take?
Twenty to forty minutes per sitting is realistic and sustainable. Consistency matters more than length: four sittings a week at twenty minutes will take you further into a book than one occasional two-hour session, mostly because the material stays warm between visits.
