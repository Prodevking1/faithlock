## Key Takeaways

- **Strictest paid mode**: Opal, whose Deep Focus session cannot be cancelled once a timer starts
- **Best strict mode for the money**: Jomo, with a multi-day lockout and anti-uninstall protection
- **Best across phone and laptop**: Freedom, which syncs one block list to iOS, Android, Mac, Windows, and the browser
- **Best Scripture-based friction**: FaithLock, where the way back into a blocked app is a Bible quiz question
- **Best gentle friction**: one sec, which shows a breathing interstitial instead of a wall
- **Best genuinely free option**: ScreenZen, free with optional donations
- **Best physical ritual**: Brick, an NFC device you tap to lock and unlock
- **The thing nobody advertises**: on iOS, every third-party blocker on this list can be weakened through Settings, Screen Time. Several developers say so themselves.

## Why Choosing an iPhone Blocker App Is Harder Than It Looks

Apple gives third-party developers one real tool for this job: the Screen Time API, part of the Family Controls framework. Almost every serious iPhone blocker app is built on it, which means they mostly share the same ceiling. What separates them is what happens at the moment you try to get back in, and how honest each company is about where its lock leaks.

That last part matters more than the feature list. AppBlock's developer replied to an App Store review confirming that revoking the app's access under Settings, Screen Time disables its Strict Mode, and said this is an iOS restriction they cannot close. Opal's own help center states that without a Screen Time Passcode and App Uninstall Protection turned on, the app can be switched off in one step. Freedom's documentation says Locked Mode by itself does not stop you from disabling Freedom's Screen Time permission unless you also set a separate passcode.

None of that makes these apps useless. It means you should pick based on which kind of friction you personally will not talk yourself out of, rather than believing any of them is unbreakable.

## How We Picked These

Four conditions, applied the same way to every app including our own:

1. **It has to run on iPhone.** Cold Turkey Blocker is one of the strictest blockers in existence, and it is not on this list because it covers Windows and macOS only, with no mobile version at all.
2. **The blocking mechanism has to be documented.** Every entry below names what it actually uses: Apple's Screen Time API, a Shortcuts interstitial, a local VPN, or hardware.
3. **The weak points have to be on the record.** Every "where it falls short" points to a named App Store review, a Trustpilot review, an independent review site, or the developer's own support documentation. Nothing here is a guess.
4. **Prices carry a date.** Screen time apps reprice often. Every figure below was checked on 2026-08-15 against the source named next to it, and should be re-checked before you buy.

## The 9 Best App Blockers for iPhone

### 1. Opal

**Best for**: People who want detailed focus analytics alongside a hard lock

Opal, from Opal OS Corporation, runs on Apple's Screen Time API on iPhone and Mac, with a Chrome extension for browser blocking. Its signature feature is Deep Focus: once you start a Deep Focus session, there is no early exit button. It layers on a Focus Score, focus reports, rewards, milestones, and a community leaderboard you can invite friends to.

**What stands out**: The analytics are deeper than most of this category offers, and Deep Focus is a genuine structural commitment rather than a dismissable prompt.

**Where it falls short**: An independent review published March 2026, checked 2026-08-15 calls the $99.99 per year Pro price steep for a screen time blocker and says the free plan is almost useless because it excludes Deep Focus. The same review found that even with Deep Focus running, users can still delete the app or change phone settings to work around it. A user on Opal's own community forum reported the app blocking apps they had not selected, an easy bypass through Screen Time settings, and a three-day struggle to get a refund after an unwanted renewal.

| Method | Apple Screen Time API, Deep Focus non-cancellable mode |

### 2. Jomo

**Best for**: A strict multi-day lockout without Opal's price tag

Jomo, from Jomo SAS, is an iOS and Mac blocker built on the Screen Time API. It blocks apps and websites across all browsers by schedule, by usage limit, or "until you take an action," and supports category-based blocking for social media, games, and shopping. Strict Mode adds an adjustable multi-day lockout with no early opt-out, plus anti-uninstall protection while it is active.

**What stands out**: Strict Mode with anti-uninstall, on-device analytics, and a stated position that it does not sell usage data. A lifetime purchase is available if you dislike subscriptions.

**Where it falls short**: Jomo maintains a dedicated troubleshooting article for blocking failing to engage or failing to release on schedule, opening with "we understand your frustration and share it." The causes it names are conflicts with other Screen Time apps, Low Power Mode, and outdated iOS or app versions. A published review found the free version too limited, with scheduled sessions, advanced groupings, and usage insights paywalled, and noted Jomo cannot block Safari at the granular level Apple's own tools allow.

| Platforms | iOS, iPad, Mac |
| Method | Apple Screen Time API |
| Anti-bypass | Strict Mode with multi-day lockout and anti-uninstall |

### 3. Freedom

**Best for**: Blocking the same sites on your phone and your laptop at the same time

Freedom, from Eighty Percent Solutions Corporation, is the broadest tool here. On iOS it combines the Screen Time API for app blocking with a local VPN profile (traffic never leaves the device) for cross-browser website blocking. It also covers Android, macOS, Windows, and Chrome, Safari, and Firefox extensions, with one synced block list across all of them.

**What stands out**: Sessions started on one device apply everywhere. Locked Mode prevents editing or stopping an active session, with one early exit allowed every seven days.

**Where it falls short**: Freedom's own support documentation states that on iOS, Locked Mode does not by itself prevent you from disabling Freedom's Screen Time permission in Settings, unless you have also configured a separate Screen Time passcode. App Store reviews report a limited block accidentally blocking "the entirety of the internet," and one case where the app made an iPhone unusable until reinstalled. Trustpilot reviews report refund difficulties and billing described as not transparent.

| Platforms | iOS, Android, macOS, Windows, browser extensions |
| Method | Screen Time API plus local VPN on iOS |
| Anti-bypass | Locked Mode; requires a separate Screen Time passcode to be airtight per Freedom's own docs |

### 4. FaithLock

**Best for**: Christians who want the unlock moment to be Scripture rather than a countdown

FaithLock blocks distracting apps through Apple's Family Controls (Screen Time) API, the same foundation Opal and Jomo use. What differs is the way back in. When you try to open a blocked app, you get a Bible verse and a quiz question about what you just read, drawn from the complete BSB (Berean Standard Bible) library of 31,000+ verses. Answer correctly and the app opens. There is no skip button on a question you have not answered.

It also has scheduled lock times for morning devotion, work hours, and bedtime, prayer reminders through the day, streak tracking with badges and a streak-freeze system, and a home screen widget showing the verse of the day.

**What stands out**: Most blockers only say no. The quiz turns the moment you reach for Instagram into thirty seconds of active recall on a passage, which is a different kind of friction from waiting out a timer you resent.

**Where it falls short**: iOS only, and there is no Android version. If you are not a Christian, the mechanism has nothing to offer you, and one of the secular options above will fit better. FaithLock is a self-accountability tool, not a parental control. It also runs on the same Screen Time API as everything else here, so it inherits the same platform-level limits Apple imposes on every third-party blocker.

| Platforms | iOS |

### 5. one sec

**Best for**: Breaking the reflex without locking yourself out

one sec, from riedel.wtf apps S.L., is not a hard restriction by default. On iOS it intercepts app launches through Shortcuts automation and shows a friction interstitial: a breathing exercise, a reflection prompt, a phone rotation. Then it asks whether you still want to open the app. There is an optional blocking mode for scheduled full restriction, plus journaling, a browser extension, and an adult content filter.

**What stands out**: It targets the impulse instead of the app. A lot of people, once asked directly, put the phone down.

**Where it falls short**: A review describes the intervention as "a speed bump, not a wall," since determined users tap through every breathing prompt without pausing. Long-term users report the pause losing its psychological effect by around week four as the novelty wears off. The free tier only fully protects one app.

### 6. ScreenZen

**Best for**: A capable blocker with no subscription at all

ScreenZen, from ScreenZen LLC, is free with optional donations. On iOS it requires Screen Time and Shortcuts permissions. Its core mechanic is a customizable delay before an app or website opens, with wait times that increase, plus per-app daily limits, session-length caps to interrupt endless scrolling, reflective prompts, and usage streaks.

**What stands out**: No mandatory subscription, and the escalating-delay design is genuinely well thought out for people whose problem is autopilot rather than intent.

**Where it falls short**: A Trustpilot reviewer states ScreenZen sends screen recordings and third-party app activity to its servers without this being mentioned in the privacy policy, and reports receiving no response from support after asking about it. Another Trustpilot reviewer reports that forgetting the app passcode after enabling uninstall prevention locks you out of your own settings until a paid unlock request is processed. An independent review site lists "can be bypassed" as a known weak point and observes the delay mechanic fades once tapping through becomes habitual.

### 7. Clearspace

**Best for**: People who want another person notified when they overshoot

Clearspace, from Clearspace Technologies, uses the Screen Time API on iOS to gate app access, then puts a forced pause screen of one to ten minutes in front of a restricted app. It offers a push-up challenge as an alternative unlock, accountability partner notifications, streak reporting, and a Strict Mode lockout.

**What stands out**: The physical unlock option is unusual, and the accountability partner feature is rare outside dedicated monitoring tools.

**Where it falls short**: An independent review flags that accountability notifications fire only after a limit is already broken, so your partner cannot approve or refuse an override in the moment, and that users can raise their own budgets whenever they like. App Store reviewers report inaccurate screen time tracking, with one describing days jumping "from one hour to 20 without opening my phone," and report the push-up challenge failing to register completed reps roughly a quarter of the time. Reviewers also call Premium expensive, and the free tier blocks only one app.

### 8. Brick

**Best for**: People who have defeated every software blocker and need an object

Brick, from Brick LLC, pairs a battery-free magnetic NFC device with a companion app. Your phone has to physically tap the Brick to start or end a session. On iOS this sits on top of system-level restrictions consistent with Screen Time. You build custom modes (Deep Work, Family Time, Sleep, Study), one Brick can pair with several phones, and the companion app carries no subscription.

**What stands out**: If you leave the Brick at home, the barrier stops being about willpower and becomes about geography. Losing it is survivable: you get five "Emergency Unbrick" uses per account, after which unlocking goes through an email request typically processed within about 48 hours.

**Where it falls short**: App Store reviewer C Parlee reports Strict Mode can be bypassed in one tap through iOS Screen Time settings. Other reviewers report scheduled unblocking failing to trigger and needing a phone restart, and the in-app timer randomly resetting to zero. An independent review notes the scheduler cannot auto-end a session after a fixed duration. The exact hardware price is not independently verified with a dated source, so check it on the manufacturer's site before ordering.

### 9. AppBlock

**Best for**: Households running a mix of iPhones and Android phones

AppBlock, from MobileSoft s.r.o. in the Czech Republic, is primarily an Android product with an iOS version. It offers Quick Block and custom schedules by time, day, location, or Wi-Fi network, an allowlist mode that blocks everything except what you choose, browser extensions for Chrome, Edge, and Brave, and a Strict Mode that normally requires the device to be on a charger or a third party's approval to unlock early.

**What stands out**: The location and Wi-Fi triggers are genuinely useful, and the lifetime purchase option avoids another subscription.

**Where it falls short**: On iPhone specifically, a user reported that revoking AppBlock's access under Settings, Screen Time disables Strict Mode entirely, and the developer confirmed in a reply that this is an iOS restriction they cannot fix. Free-tier caps are tight on iOS (a 4-hour Quick Block plus two schedules). Reviewers report notifications reappearing several times an hour after being disabled, pauses taking up to 30 seconds to apply, and the app "forgetting" a paid subscription until restarted.

## How to Choose Your iPhone Blocker App

**You want the hardest lock and will pay for it**: Opal's Deep Focus or Jomo's Strict Mode. Jomo costs meaningfully less for a comparable feature.

**You get distracted on a laptop too**: Freedom. Nothing else here syncs one block list across phone and desktop.

**You want the block to point somewhere**: FaithLock, if a Bible verse and a question about it is a form of friction you would actually welcome rather than resent.

**You do not want to be locked out, only slowed down**: one sec.

**You refuse to pay anything**: ScreenZen.

**Software has never worked for you**: Brick, and leave the device in another room.

**You are on Android too**: AppBlock, which is stronger there than on iPhone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the best iPhone blocker app in 2026?**
There is no single answer, because the apps differ by mechanism rather than quality. Opal and Jomo have the strictest lockouts, Freedom is the only one covering phone and computer together, ScreenZen is the strongest free option, and FaithLock is the one built around Scripture engagement. Pick by which friction you will not disable at 11pm.

**Can any iPhone blocker app be bypassed?**
Yes, and the honest developers say so. Opal's help center, Freedom's support docs, and AppBlock's developer all confirm that a user can weaken or disable their app through Settings, Screen Time. The standard mitigation is setting a Screen Time passcode you do not know, which usually means asking someone else to set it.

**Is there a free app blocker for iPhone that actually works?**
ScreenZen is free with optional donations and is the only tool here without a mandatory paid tier. Apple's own Screen Time is also free, though its "Ignore Limit" button undermines it for most people. one sec's free tier fully protects a single app.

**Do these apps drain iPhone battery?**
Apps built on Apple's Screen Time API run at the system level and have minimal impact. Freedom and Canopy-style tools that install a VPN profile are the heavier category, and Canopy in particular draws battery and data complaints in App Store reviews.

**Can I use an iPhone blocker app for my kid?**
Most of these are self-accountability tools, not parental controls, and a determined teenager will find Settings, Screen Time. For genuine parental supervision, see our guide to the [best Christian parental control apps](/resources/best-christian-parental-control-apps).

## Final Thoughts

If I had to hand one recommendation to someone who has never installed a blocker: start with Jomo. It gives you the strictest realistic lockout on iPhone for a fraction of Opal's price, and its developers publish a troubleshooting article admitting where it breaks, which I trust more than silence.

If your problem is not really time but where your attention goes when you put the phone down, a countdown timer is the wrong tool and no amount of strictness will fix that. That is the gap FaithLock is built for, and it is also the reason it will not suit everyone.

Whichever you pick, set a Screen Time passcode you do not control. Every app on this list is stronger with one, and several of them are close to decorative without one.
