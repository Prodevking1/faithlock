## There Is No Quiet Time and That Is Not Your Fault

Every devotional aimed at mothers seems to assume a window that does not exist. Rise before the children. Find a quiet corner. Spend thirty focused minutes. This advice is written for a season of life, and it is not the season where someone is awake at 5:20 anyway, or where the baby who was going to sleep through the night has decided otherwise, or where you finally sit down and someone needs help with something in the bathroom.

The most useful thing to say to a mother of young children is this: the interruption is not preventing your devotional life. It is the conditions of it. God is not waiting for you to get an hour free before anything counts.

So these seven readings are short, and they are designed to be read in pieces. If you get through half of one and someone needs you, that half counted.

## Reading 1: God Adjusts His Pace for You

> "He tends his flock like a shepherd: He gathers the lambs in his arms and carries them close to his heart; he gently leads those that have young." (Isaiah 40:11, NIV)

The last clause is easy to read past and it is the whole verse for a mother. "He gently leads those that have young." A shepherd moving a flock with newborn animals cannot move at the pace of the healthy adults. He slows to the speed of the slowest, and that slowing is not resentful. It is what shepherding is.

Whatever season you are in where you cannot keep the pace you used to keep, this is the verse. God is not standing at the front of the flock waiting impatiently for you to catch up with your old spiritual routine.

**Carry this:** You are being led gently and slowly on purpose. The slow pace is the accommodation, not the failure.

## Reading 2: They Were Given, Not Assigned as Punishment

> "Children are a heritage from the LORD, offspring a reward from him." (Psalm 127:3, NIV)

On a difficult afternoon this verse can land like an accusation, as if you should be feeling grateful and are not. Read it again with the emphasis on the direction of the gift rather than on your obligation to feel a certain way.

A heritage is something received, and something you did not manufacture. On the days when parenting feels entirely like output, this verse insists that something was given to you before anything was required of you. That is true even on the afternoons when it does not feel like it, and feelings are not the evidence being cited here.

**Carry this:** You do not have to feel grateful for this to be true. Truth does not require your mood to cooperate.

## Reading 3: There Is a Floor Underneath You

> "The eternal God is your refuge, and underneath are the everlasting arms…" (Deuteronomy 33:27a, NIV)

"Underneath" is a specific direction. Not around you, not ahead of you. Underneath, which is where you look when you are afraid of falling further than you already have.

Mothers of young children describe a particular fear that things are one bad night away from coming apart. This verse does not promise the bad night will not come. It says there is a floor, and the floor is not your own resilience, which is a relief on the days when your resilience is clearly gone.

**Carry this:** When you catch yourself calculating how much more you can take, say "underneath are the everlasting arms." That is a floor you did not build and cannot exhaust.

## Reading 4: Weakness Is Where This Works

> "But he said to me, 'My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness.' Therefore I will boast all the more gladly about my weaknesses, so that Christ's power may rest on me." (2 Corinthians 12:9, NIV)

Paul asked three times to have a specific weakness removed, and God said no. That is worth noting before anything else. This is not a verse about weakness that gets fixed.

"Made perfect in weakness" means the weakness is the location where the power operates, not an obstacle to it. A mother running on five broken hours of sleep is not in a compromised spiritual position. According to this verse, she is in the precise position where God's strength is most visible, which is an inversion of everything else she is being told.

**Carry this:** Your depleted state is not disqualifying you from anything. Say once today: "I do not have enough, and that is where this works."

## Reading 5: The Burden Is Carried Daily, Not Once

> "Praise be to the Lord, to God our Savior, who daily bears our burdens." (Psalm 68:19, NIV)

"Daily" is the operative word. Not once, not at the beginning, not in a single dramatic rescue. Daily, which matches the actual rhythm of a mother's life, where the same tasks return every twenty-four hours and nothing stays done.

There is something appropriate about a God whose burden-bearing is on the same schedule as the laundry. The dishes come back, the meals come back, the bedtime resistance comes back, and so does this.

**Carry this:** Ask once today, in the middle of something repetitive, for the burden of that exact task to be carried. Not the big things. That one.

## Reading 6: The Children Are Not the Interruption

> "When Jesus saw this, he was indignant. He said to them, 'Let the little children come to me, and do not hinder them, for the kingdom of God belongs to such as these.'" (Mark 10:14, NIV)

The disciples were doing what seemed obviously right. Jesus was teaching adults about serious matters and the children were a disturbance, so they intervened. Jesus was indignant, which is a strong word and it is aimed at the people trying to keep the important work uninterrupted.

The moment a mother resents her children for interrupting her spiritual life is a moment worth examining against this passage. The disciples had a category error about what counted as the real work. It is an easy error to repeat at 7 AM with an open Bible and a child who has appeared next to your chair.

**Carry this:** The next interruption is not competing with your devotional. Let the child come.

## Reading 7: Nobody Is Watching and It Still Counts

> "Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as working for the Lord, not for human masters, since you know that you will receive an inheritance from the Lord as a reward. It is the Lord Christ you are serving." (Colossians 3:23-24, NIV)

Paul wrote this to household servants doing repetitive, unrecognized work that nobody would ever record. It applies almost exactly to the invisible labor of running a home: the thousand small acts that are noticed only when they stop.

"Not for human masters" reframes the audience. The work is not being evaluated by the people who benefit from it, most of whom are too young to notice. It is being done for someone who does notice, and the recognition is described as an inheritance rather than a thank-you.

**Carry this:** Pick one unnoticed task today and do it deliberately as an offering. Same task, different audience.

## How to Keep This Going in a Season With No Margin

**Redefine the unit.** One verse is a devotional. If your standard is a chapter and a journal, this dies in a week. If your standard is one verse, it survives years, including the hard ones.

**Use the fragments you have.** Nursing. Waiting in the pickup line. The four minutes while something is in the microwave. You will not find a block. You already have twelve fragments and most of them currently go to your phone.

**Let it be audible.** Reading a verse out loud with children in the room is not a compromised version. It is teaching, prayer, and your own devotional at the same time.

**Put the verse where you already look.** You check your phone constantly, often one-handed while holding a child. Setting a [Bible verse as your wallpaper](/resources/bible-verses-for-wallpapers) puts Scripture into a hundred glances you are already making.

**Be honest about the phone.** For a lot of mothers the phone is the only thing that feels like it belongs to them, and that is understandable rather than shameful. It is also true that an hour of scrolling leaves most people more depleted than they started. A [Christian app blocker](/resources/best-christian-app-blocker) that requires a Bible verse before opening a feed does not take the break away; it changes what fills it.

**Stop measuring against other seasons.** The devotional life you had before children was built in different conditions. Comparing them produces guilt and no useful information.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How can a busy mom have a quiet time?**
Mostly by abandoning the word "quiet." Reading one verse aloud with children present, in fragments, across the day, is a legitimate devotional practice. Waiting for silence in a season that does not have silence usually means waiting for years.

**What is a good devotional for moms of young children?**
A very short one with a single verse and one thought to carry. Devotionals designed around a thirty minute block are built for a different season and tend to produce guilt rather than habit in this one.

**Is it wrong to feel touched out and resentful?**
No, and admitting it is generally healthier than suppressing it. Scripture is full of people telling God exactly how depleted they are. Psalm 6, Psalm 13, and much of Lamentations are written by people at the end of their capacity.

**How do I stop scrolling during the only break I get?**
Replace it rather than removing it. A break with nothing in it will be filled by the phone by default. Deciding in advance what the ten minutes is for, even if the answer is "sitting down and one verse," changes the outcome. Our guide on [phone addiction](/resources/how-to-stop-phone-addiction-christian) covers the practical steps.

**What if I fall asleep every time I try to pray?**
Then you are tired, which is information rather than a spiritual problem. Pray with your eyes open while doing something with your hands. Many mothers find the dishes are a better prayer setting than a chair.

**How do I teach my children faith when I barely have my own routine?**
Out loud, in fragments, in front of them. Children absorb far more from a mother saying one verse at breakfast than from a devotional routine they never see. Your fragmented practice is their formation.
