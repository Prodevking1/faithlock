## Nobody Is Checking on You Now

The thing nobody warns you about before college is not the workload. It is the sudden and total absence of external structure. For eighteen years something told you when to be somewhere. Now nothing does. You could not attend a church for four years and no one in your life would necessarily notice.

That is the actual spiritual condition of college, and it is why faith either deepens sharply or evaporates quietly during these years. Very little happens in the middle. The students who come out the other side with something intact are usually not the ones with the best arguments. They are the ones who built a structure when nothing required them to.

These seven readings are about that. Each is short, because you have reading assignments already.

## Reading 1: Decide Before You Are in the Room

> "But Daniel resolved not to defile himself with the royal food and wine, and he asked the chief official for permission not to defile himself this way." (Daniel 1:8, NIV)

Daniel is a teenager who has been deported to Babylon and enrolled in what amounts to an elite training program. This is a study abroad situation with much higher stakes, and the first thing recorded about him is a decision made in advance.

"Resolved" happens before the food arrives. That is the whole mechanism. Daniel is not standing at the table weighing options with everyone watching; he settled the question privately, when it was cheap to settle, so that the public moment was merely execution.

**This week:** Pick the one situation you know is coming this semester and decide now what you will do. Write it down. Deciding in the room almost never goes well.

## Reading 2: You Are Going to Meet Persuasive Ideas

> "So then, just as you received Christ Jesus as Lord, continue to live your lives in him, rooted and built up in him, strengthened in the faith as you were taught, and overflowing with thankfulness. See to it that no one takes you captive through hollow and deceptive philosophy, which depends on human tradition and the elemental spiritual forces of this world rather than on Christ." (Colossians 2:6-8, NIV)

"Takes you captive" describes something that happens gradually and without consent. Paul is not describing an argument you lose. He is describing being carried off, which is closer to how it actually works: a semester of one framework presented confidently, and your assumptions have shifted before you evaluated anything.

The defense he gives is not counter-arguments. It is being "rooted and built up," which is structural. A student who knows what they believe and why is not immune to hard questions, but they engage them rather than absorb them.

**This week:** Write down, in your own words, what you actually believe about one contested thing. If you cannot write it, you have not thought it, and an unarticulated belief is easy to take captive.

## Reading 3: Your Friends Are Your Trajectory

> "Walk with the wise and become wise, for a companion of fools suffers harm." (Proverbs 13:20, NIV)

The verb is "become." Not learn from, not resemble. Become. Proverbs treats proximity as formative rather than optional, which is a harder claim than it first sounds.

College is the last time in your life when you choose your entire social circle from scratch in a single week. Most students make that choice based on geography, whoever lived on the same hall in September, and then live with the consequences for four years. It is worth being more deliberate than that.

**This week:** Look at the five people you spend the most time with. Ask honestly whether you are becoming more like them, and whether that is a direction you would choose.

## Reading 4: Doubt Out Loud Is Better Than Doubt in Private

> "Immediately the boy's father exclaimed, 'I do believe; help me overcome my unbelief!'" (Mark 9:24, NIV)

This is one of the most honest sentences in the Gospels, and it is a contradiction. The man says he believes and asks for help with not believing, in the same breath. Jesus does not correct him or ask him to resolve it first. He answers the request.

Most college students hold both of those at once and think it disqualifies them. It does not. What damages faith in these years is rarely the doubt itself. It is doubting silently for three years because you assume saying it out loud would be a scandal.

**This week:** Say your actual doubt out loud to one person who will not panic. If you do not have that person, finding them is the assignment.

## Reading 5: Run From Something, Run Toward Someone

> "Flee the evil desires of youth and pursue righteousness, faith, love and peace, along with those who call on the Lord out of a pure heart." (2 Timothy 2:22, NIV)

Note the final clause. This is not a solo instruction. You pursue these things "along with those who call on the Lord," which makes the pursuit a group activity by design.

That clause is doing more work than the rest of the verse for a college student. Fleeing anything alone, in a dorm, at 1 AM, with nobody expecting anything from you, is a losing proposition. Paul assumes companionship is part of the method rather than a nice addition to it.

**This week:** Find one person who will ask you a direct question about how you are actually doing. Ask them to do it weekly.

## Reading 6: Choose Deliberately or Drift by Default

> "But if serving the LORD seems undesirable to you, then choose for yourselves this day whom you will serve, whether the gods your ancestors served beyond the Euphrates, or the gods of the Amorites, in whose land you are living. But as for me and my household, we will serve the LORD." (Joshua 24:15, NIV)

Joshua puts two categories on the table: the gods of your ancestors and the gods of the land you are currently living in. That is a remarkably precise description of a college student's options. You have an inherited faith from home and a set of assumptions native to the place you now live.

His point is that not choosing is not neutral. If you do not decide, the land you are living in decides for you, because its gods are the default and they are all around you.

**This week:** Decide whether the faith you have is yours or your parents'. It is a real question and it deserves an actual answer rather than four years of postponement.

## Reading 7: Do Not Stop Showing Up

> "And let us consider how we may spur one another on toward love and good deeds, not giving up meeting together, as some are in the habit of doing, but encouraging one another—and all the more as you see the Day approaching." (Hebrews 10:24-25, NIV)

"As some are in the habit of doing" tells you this problem is not new. People have been quietly stopping attendance for two thousand years, and the writer names it as a habit, which is exactly what it is. Nobody decides to stop going to church. They miss one Sunday, then two, and the missing becomes normal.

"Spur one another on" is a deliberately uncomfortable image. A spur is not gentle. The assumption is that you will need prodding and that you will not generate it yourself, which is an accurate assessment of most people at 9 AM on a Sunday.

**This week:** Go once. Not as a commitment to a lifetime of Sundays. Once, this week, and then decide about next week next week.

## How to Build Structure When Nothing Requires It

**Put it on the calendar like a class.** Free time expands to fill available space, and a devotional that happens "when I get a chance" does not happen. Give it a slot with a start time.

**Use the gaps you already have.** The forty minutes between classes, the wait for the dining hall, the bus. You are not going to find an hour. You already have four fifteen-minute blocks and they are currently going to your phone.

**Get one accountability person in your first month.** Not a group of eight that dissolves by October. One person. The failure rate on this is much lower than it is on solitary discipline.

**Do it during exams too, especially during exams.** Every student drops their habits during finals and then never restarts them. Cutting to three minutes during a heavy week is far better than pausing for three weeks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I keep my faith in college?**
Build structure deliberately, since nothing external will provide it, and find at least one person who expects to hear from you. Students who lose their faith in college usually describe drift rather than a decision, and structure plus community is what interrupts drift.

**What is a good devotional for college students?**
A short one you can do in a gap between classes. Five minutes with one passage beats an elaborate plan you abandon in week three. Consistency during a chaotic schedule matters more than depth.

**Is it normal to doubt my faith in college?**
Yes, and it is close to universal among students who are actually thinking. The concerning pattern is not doubt but silence about doubt. Read Mark 9:24 again and find someone to say it to.

**How do I find a church at school?**
Visit three in your first month and then pick one before you have a perfect answer. Students who keep searching for the ideal church usually attend none. Choosing an adequate one early is much better than choosing an excellent one in March.

**How do I stay focused when studying with my phone right there?**
Put it in another room or block the specific apps during study blocks. Willpower against a device engineered for engagement is a poor strategy, and physical or software friction works better. Our guide to [stopping doomscrolling](/resources/best-stop-doomscrolling-apps) covers the available tools.

**What if I have not been to church in a semester?**
Go once this week and do not construct a narrative about it first. The gap feels much larger from the inside than it looks to anyone else.
