## Ten Minutes, Both of You, Out Loud

Most couples who want to do a devotional together fail at logistics, not spirituality. One of you is a morning person and one is not. One of you wants to discuss it and the other wants to finish it. You start a plan in January, miss four days in February, and quietly stop mentioning it.

So these seven readings are built around a realistic format: about ten minutes, read aloud by one of you, ending with one thing to actually say to each other. Not a study. Not a conversation that requires both of you to be emotionally available at 10 PM. One verse, a short reflection, one sentence exchanged.

A warning that will save you some grief: do not use these as ammunition. If you read the section on anger and immediately think of your spouse rather than yourself, you are holding it backwards.

## Reading 1: Two Is Structurally Better Than One

> "Two are better than one, because they have a good return for their labor: If either of them falls down, one can help the other up. But pity anyone who falls and has no one to help them up." (Ecclesiastes 4:9-10, NIV)

> "Though one may be overpowered, two can defend themselves. A cord of three strands is not quickly broken." (Ecclesiastes 4:12, NIV)

The argument here is unromantic and practical. Two people produce a better return, survive falls, and resist being overpowered. Solomon is not describing a feeling. He is describing load-bearing capacity.

The third strand is usually read as God, and that reading holds. But notice the mechanism: three strands are not stronger because the third is thicker. They are stronger because a load applied to one gets distributed across all three. A marriage carrying weight through only two strands is not immoral. It is just under more tension than it needs to be.

**Say to each other tonight:** One thing you have been carrying alone this month that the other person did not know about.

## Reading 2: Leaving Is Part of Joining

> "That is why a man leaves his father and mother and is united to his wife, and they become one flesh." (Genesis 2:24, NIV)

"Leaves" comes before "united." That order gets skipped constantly, and it is not only about geography or in-laws. It is about which relationship gets the final word.

Plenty of couples are still emotionally reporting to a parent, a group of friends, or the family they grew up in. The habits, expectations, and grievance patterns of the old household get imported wholesale and then defended as if they were fixed features of reality. The instruction is to leave those, not just the house.

**Say to each other tonight:** One expectation you brought from the family you grew up in that you have never actually discussed.

## Reading 3: Patience Is Bearing With, Not Waiting Out

> "Be completely humble and gentle; be patient, bearing with one another in love. Make every effort to keep the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace." (Ephesians 4:2-3, NIV)

"Bearing with one another" assumes there is something to bear. Paul is not describing two compatible people enjoying each other. He is describing the ordinary friction of being close to someone whose habits cost you something.

"Make every effort" is the phrase to notice. Unity is not the natural resting state of two people sharing a bathroom and a bank account. Left alone, couples drift. The verse assumes effort is required, and normalizes the fact that it is.

**Say to each other tonight:** One small thing the other person does that you have been silently tolerating. Say it gently, and then let it go rather than negotiating it.

## Reading 4: Love as a List of Behaviors

> "Love is patient, love is kind. It does not envy, it does not boast, it is not proud. It does not dishonor others, it is not self-seeking, it is not easily angered, it keeps no record of wrongs." (1 Corinthians 13:4-5, NIV)

Read this one substituting your own name for "love" and notice where it stops being true. That exercise is uncomfortable and it is the fastest way to get value out of this passage.

"Keeps no record of wrongs" is the line most couples fail. Every long relationship accumulates a ledger, and in an argument the ledger comes out with dates attached. Paul says love does not maintain the file. Not that it forgets, but that it stops updating the record.

**Say to each other tonight:** One item from your ledger that you are choosing to close. Name it, then say it is closed.

## Reading 5: Forgiving at the Scale You Were Forgiven

> "Bear with each other and forgive one another if any of you has a grievance against someone. Forgive as the Lord forgave you." (Colossians 3:13, NIV)

The standard is set at the end: "as the Lord forgave you." Not proportionally to the offense. Not once an apology meets your specification. The measure is what you have already received.

Most marital forgiveness stalls because it is conditional on the other person understanding exactly how much they hurt you. That condition can go unmet for years. Paul removes it and replaces it with a comparison you cannot win an argument against.

**Say to each other tonight:** One thing you have forgiven but never told them you forgave. They may not know it is settled.

## Reading 6: Do Not Take the Argument to Bed

> "In your anger do not sin: Do not let the sun go down while you are still angry, and do not give the devil a foothold." (Ephesians 4:26-27, NIV)

Paul does not tell couples not to be angry. He puts a deadline on it. Anger is permitted, an overnight stay is not.

"Foothold" is the operative word. An unresolved argument does not stay the same size overnight. It gets a foothold, which means it becomes the interpretive frame for the next morning's ordinary interaction. The tone of a breakfast question gets read through last night's fight. That is how a specific disagreement turns into a general climate.

**Do this tonight:** If something is unresolved right now, name it before you sleep. You do not have to solve it. Saying "we are not finished with this and I am still with you" is enough to deny the foothold.

## Reading 7: Attention Is the Thing You Are Actually Giving

> "Do nothing out of selfish ambition or vain conceit. Rather, in humility value others above yourselves, not looking to your own interests but each of you to the interests of the others." (Philippians 2:3-4, NIV)

"Not looking to your own interests" is a statement about where the eyes go. In practice, for most couples in this decade, the eyes go to a screen. Two people on a sofa, physically together, attending to entirely separate things for three hours.

Nobody decides to do this. It accumulates. But attention is the most honest measure of what you value above yourself, because unlike affection, it can be counted. Look at the hours.

**Do this tonight:** Put both phones in another room for one hour and stay in the same room. Notice how long it takes before one of you reaches for a pocket.

## How Couples Actually Sustain This

**Pick a time neither of you resents.** Not 6 AM if one of you is not human until 8. Not 10:30 PM if the other one is asleep by 10. The right slot is the one where you are both mildly willing, which is usually right after dinner or right before bed.

**One person reads out loud.** Silent reading side by side is not a shared activity. Reading aloud is, and it also prevents one of you from skimming.

**Keep it to ten minutes.** Ambitious couples plan forty-five minute studies and complete two. Ten minutes, every day, is a habit. Forty-five minutes, occasionally, is an event that keeps getting cancelled.

**Do not skip the sentence at the end.** The reading is the easy part. The sentence you say to each other is where anything actually happens. If you only have time for one, do the sentence.

**Take the phones out of the room.** A devotional interrupted twice by notifications is not a devotional. Some couples find it easier to block distracting apps during a set window rather than relying on both people to resist, which is what a [Christian app blocker](/resources/best-christian-app-blocker) is for.

**Miss days together.** If one of you decides a missed day is a failure, this dies. Two nights a week done properly beats seven attempted and resented.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a good devotional for couples to do together?**
A short one, read aloud, that ends with something specific to say to each other. Length is the most common failure point. Couples abandon long plans far more often than short ones, so the plan you can finish on a tired Tuesday is the right plan.

**How long should a couples devotional take?**
Ten to fifteen minutes including the conversation at the end. If it regularly runs past thirty, one of you is likely to start dreading it, which defeats the purpose.

**What if only one of us wants to do this?**
Then do it alone and do not campaign. A devotional used as leverage becomes another point of conflict. Read our [daily devotional for women](/resources/daily-devotional-for-women) or [daily devotional for men](/resources/daily-devotional-for-men) on your own, and let the change in you be the argument.

**Should we do this in the morning or at night?**
Night works better for most couples simply because it is the hour both people are in the same room. Morning works if you already share breakfast. Choose based on your actual schedule rather than an ideal one.

**What if a reading starts an argument?**
Stop the reading and have the argument, then come back. A devotional that surfaces a real issue has done something useful. The failure mode is not conflict, it is finishing the page while both of you privately seethe.

**How do we handle phones during our devotional time?**
Physically remove them. Leaving them face down on the table does not work, because the reach is automatic. Another room, or blocked apps for that window, is what actually holds.
