## Key Takeaways

- There is no single best porn blocker, and the question is usually mis-framed. The useful question is which failure you keep repeating, because each failure has a different mechanism attached to it.
- If you have already tried and abandoned a filter, the reason is almost always one of three things: you kept the passcode, the coverage missed where it actually happened, or the tool asked for daily effort.
- The strongest configuration for one adult's own phone costs nothing and involves another person. That is not a cost saving tip, it is the mechanism.
- For a child's device, start with the platform's own parental controls, not with a purchase.

## Choose by Situation, Not by Rating

| Your situation | The mechanism that fits | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Your own phone, and you keep undoing your own settings | OS content restriction, passcode held by someone else | Removes your ability to reverse it alone |
| Work laptop you do not administer | Browser extension, plus filtering DNS if permitted | The only layers you can install without admin rights |
| Whole household, many devices | Filtering DNS at the router | One change reaches every device on the network |
| A child's phone | Platform parental controls first, third party filter only if those fall short | Free, deeply integrated, and reversible |
| You do not want a wall, you want to stop hiding | Accountability software with a real partner | Blocking and disclosure solve different problems |
| It happens when bored, late, or idle rather than deliberately | App blocking on the apps that carry the boredom | The trigger is the app habit, not the browser |

Read the row that matches you, then the matching section below. Skipping to a product name is how people end up buying the wrong mechanism twice.

## If You Keep Undoing Your Own Settings

This is the most common pattern, and the most commonly misdiagnosed. People conclude that the filter was too weak, and buy a stricter one, which they also disable.

The variable that predicts whether a filter survives is not its strictness. It is whether you can reverse it by yourself. Both platforms already ship a strong enough filter, so the money is better spent on nothing at all and the effort spent on the awkward conversation instead.

**Concretely:** turn on the built-in web content restriction, then have someone else set the passcode and not tell you. That single step converts a setting you control into a setting you do not, and it is free. Details in [how to block adult content on iPhone](/resources/how-to-block-adult-content-on-iphone).

If nobody in your life can hold that passcode today, an accountability product is the substitute, because it produces the same effect through a different route: someone else finds out. Not as good, and available immediately.

## If the Coverage Missed Where It Actually Happened

The second pattern. The filter worked, and it simply was not standing where the thing happened.

Browser extensions cover one browser on one computer. SafeSearch covers Google Search and, in Google's own words, "doesn't affect content on other search engines or websites" ([Google Search Help](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/510), checked 2026-08-16). Router DNS covers everything at home and nothing on cellular data. Covenant Eyes on iPhone filters device wide through its VPN, but its screenshot reporting covers Safari only, so other browsers and apps stay outside the report ([TechLockdown](https://www.techlockdown.com/articles/covenant-eyes-iphone-review), checked 2026-08-16).

**Concretely:** before buying anything, write down where it actually happens. Which device, which app, what time of day. Then pick the mechanism that stands in that exact spot. Most disappointment in this category is a coverage mismatch that a single honest sentence would have prevented.

For whole device coverage on a phone, network level filtering is the answer, which in practice means a filtering DNS profile or a product like Canopy that runs a network extension.

## If the Tool Asked for Daily Effort

Anything requiring you to remember it will fail eventually, because the moment it matters is the moment you are least inclined to cooperate with your own plan.

**Concretely:** prefer mechanisms that are on by default and invisible when working. Router DNS qualifies. OS restrictions qualify. An extension you have to keep enabled does not.

## If You Are Setting This Up for a Child

Start free and start native. On Android, Family Link's Chrome filtering offers "Try to block explicit sites", with the caveat Google states itself: "No filter is perfect, but this should help hide sexually explicit sites" ([Google For Families Help](https://support.google.com/families/answer/7087030), checked 2026-08-16). On iPhone, Screen Time's Web Content setting offers Limit Adult Websites, reached through Content & Privacy Restrictions and then App Store, Media, Web, & Games ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/105121), checked 2026-08-16).

Move to a paid product only when you have identified something specific the native controls miss. Canopy is the strongest technical option for that, because it filters at the network level and blocks competing VPNs, though its App Store rating sits around 3.8 with recurring reports of overblocking and slowdown ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/canopy-ai-online-safety-app/id1492266682), checked 2026-08-16). Bark takes the opposite approach, alerting a parent rather than building walls, and on iOS its monitoring is periodic because it depends on a physical accessory ([Bark support](https://support.bark.us/en/articles/13928365-how-to-monitor-ios-devices-with-the-bark-sync), checked 2026-08-16).

A word on the relationship, since it determines whether any of this holds: a filter installed without explanation tends to produce concealment rather than change, particularly with teenagers. The tool works better when the person it is installed on knows why.

## If What You Want Is Not a Block

Some people do not need the door locked. They need to stop being the only one who knows.

That is accountability software, a genuinely different product category that permits access and reports it. Accountable2You states outright that it is not a blocking tool, and allows unlimited partners per device. Covenant Eyes builds its whole model around a report to a chosen partner.

It fails in exactly one way, and it is worth naming before you buy: it depends entirely on somebody reading the report and saying something. Choose the person before choosing the product. [Choosing an accountability partner](/resources/christian-accountability-partner) covers what makes that work and what makes it collapse.

## Where an App Habit Is the Real Trigger

For some people the pattern is not intent but idleness: a reach for the phone with nothing in mind, and the rest follows. Filtering does not address that, because there was never a decision for the filter to intercept.

[FaithLock](/) sits here, and only here. It is an iOS app that shields apps you choose using Apple's Family Controls API, and reopening one requires reading a Bible verse and answering a fill-in-the-blank question about it, drawn from the complete Berean Standard Bible library of 31,000 or more verses. It is worth being exact about what that is not: **it is not a content filter and it does not report to anyone.** If your problem is filtering, everything above is more relevant.

## When Software Is Not the Answer

If you have run several of these attempts and the pattern has not moved, and it is affecting sleep, work, or a relationship, the limiting factor is not which tool you chose. The [Psychology Today therapist directory](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/therapists) filters by concern and location. If any of this involves thoughts of self-harm or a crisis, that comes first: the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/) is free, 24/7, by call, text, or chat.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the best porn blocker for iPhone?**
For an adult's own phone, the built-in Screen Time restriction with the passcode held by someone else, which is free. For a device where you need network level filtering and cannot rely on a passcode holder, Canopy is the strongest technical option, with the trade-offs its own reviewers describe. See [porn blocker for iPhone](/resources/porn-blocker-iphone).

**What is the best porn blocker that cannot be turned off?**
None of them, on a personal device. The closest realistic version is a filter whose passcode you do not know, which relocates the defense from software to a person. Products that make removal harder still fall to whoever holds the credentials.

**Is a paid porn blocker better than a free one?**
For blocking web content, usually not, since the underlying mechanism is often identical. Paid products buy reporting, tamper alerts, image level classification and support. Decide which of those you need before paying for all four.

**What is the best porn blocker for a family?**
Filtering DNS at the router as the base layer, because it reaches every device including televisions and consoles, then per device parental controls for phones that leave the house on cellular data. Add a monitoring product only if you specifically want alerts.

**Which porn blocker works on apps and not just websites?**
Anything filtering at the network level, meaning DNS filters and filtering VPN products such as Canopy or Covenant Eyes. Browser extensions and SafeSearch do not reach apps at all.
