## Key Statistics

**Teens (ages 13-18) average 8 hours and 39 minutes of screen media use per day, and tweens (ages 8-12) average 5 hours and 33 minutes**, according to Common Sense Media's Census: Media Use by Tweens and Teens, a nationally representative survey published March 23, 2022. Both figures represent a 17% increase from the same census two years earlier.

**38% of U.S. teens say they spend too much time on their smartphone**, according to Pew Research Center's report "How Teens and Parents Approach Screen Time," published March 11, 2024, based on a survey of teens ages 13-17.

**44% of teens say not having their smartphone makes them feel anxious**, per the same Pew Research Center report. The number rises to 55% among girls ages 15-17.

**47% of U.S. teens ages 13-17 say they are online "almost constantly,"** up from 24% a decade earlier, according to Pew Research Center's "Teens, Social Media and Technology 2024" report, published December 12, 2024, based on a survey conducted September 18 to October 10, 2024.

**The average American checks their phone 186 times per day, about 11.6 times per hour, roughly once every 5 minutes of waking time**, according to a Reviews.org survey of roughly 1,000 U.S. adults conducted in Q4 2025. The same survey found Americans spend an average of 3 hours and 14 minutes on their phones daily, and 46% consider themselves "addicted" to their phone.

**58% of Americans look at screens within an hour of going to bed**, according to the National Sleep Foundation's 2022 Sleep in America Poll.

## What the Numbers Mean

The gap between tween and teen screen time (5:33 versus 8:39) tracks with independence, not just interest. Teens carry personal phones, manage their own social accounts, and face fewer parental checkpoints than younger children. The 17% jump between 2019 and 2021 also lines up with the pandemic years, when school, socializing, and entertainment all moved onto screens at once, and the Common Sense data shows those higher numbers held rather than falling back afterward.

The Pew numbers on teen anxiety point to something worth sitting with: 44% of teens say the absence of their phone makes them anxious, not the presence of a problem on it. That's a dependency signal more than a content signal. It suggests the phone itself, regardless of what's happening on the screen, has become something teens feel unsettled without.

The 186 daily phone checks reported by Reviews.org works out to roughly one check every 5 minutes across waking hours. Very little of that is a deliberate decision each time. Most checks are reflexive, a habit loop built by notifications, boredom, and the phone's constant physical presence, not 186 separate choices to seek out something specific.

The bedtime figure matters because of what happens biologically once a screen is in view. Blue light exposure in the evening suppresses melatonin production, and the National Sleep Foundation's research consistently links pre-sleep screen use to lower sleep quality scores. More than half of Americans are doing this in the hour that matters most for winding down.

## The Trend Over Time

Common Sense Media has tracked tween and teen screen time since 2015, when teens averaged about 9 hours of entertainment media daily under a broader measurement approach. The 2021 census, using a comparable methodology to the 2019 wave, showed the pandemic-era jump from 7:22 to 8:39 for teens. That increase has not reversed in the years since school and social life returned to in-person settings.

Pew's tracking of "almost constant" internet use among teens shows a steady climb rather than a plateau: 24% in 2014-15, and 47% by the 2024 survey. Roughly doubling over a decade suggests the behavior isn't a temporary spike tied to any single app or platform, but a shift in how continuously teens expect to stay connected.

Adult phone-checking frequency has also climbed. Reviews.org's own year-over-year tracking has shown rising check counts as notification volume, app design, and habitual use have all intensified. The screen time conversation has moved from "how much time on apps" to a more granular question: how many separate interruptions does a phone create across a single day.

## What Christians Should Know

Scripture doesn't condemn technology, but it repeatedly calls believers to be intentional with attention and time. Ephesians 5:15-16 urges believers to make the most of every opportunity, "because the days are evil." A teen or adult checking a phone 186 times a day, or spending 8-plus hours in screen media, has very little of that time left as deliberate, examined choice.

The anxiety data is worth particular attention for parents and pastors. When 44% of teens report feeling anxious without their phone, that's describing something closer to dependency than preference. First Corinthians 6:12 speaks directly to this: "I have the right to do anything, but not everything is beneficial. I have the right to do anything, but I will not be mastered by anything." A tool that produces anxiety in its absence has, functionally, become a master.

The bedtime statistic connects to a specific spiritual practice worth reclaiming: an unhurried transition from the day into rest, without the last input before sleep being a feed engineered to keep attention. Psalm 4:8 describes lying down in peace because of God's presence, not because every notification has been checked and cleared.

Practical steps that follow from this data: setting a hard stop for screens 30-60 minutes before bed, treating high check-frequency as a signal to examine rather than a normal baseline, and building in moments, morning, evening, or before opening a distracting app, where Scripture interrupts habit before habit runs the day uninterrupted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much screen time is too much for teens?**
There's no single medically agreed cutoff, but the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends consistent limits and screen-free times (like meals and before bed) rather than a specific hourly ceiling. The more useful question for most families is whether screen time is displacing sleep, in-person relationships, or schoolwork, which the current averages (8:39 daily for teens) increasingly do.

**What percent of Americans check their phone too often?**
"Too often" isn't formally defined, but the Reviews.org data showing 186 checks per day, and 46% of adults self-identifying as addicted to their phones, suggests a large share of the population would describe their own usage as excessive if asked directly.

**Does screen time before bed actually affect sleep?**
Yes. The National Sleep Foundation's research links pre-sleep screen exposure to measurably lower sleep quality scores, largely due to blue light suppressing melatonin and stimulating content keeping the mind active past the point it should be winding down.

**Has screen time gone up or down since the pandemic?**
It has largely stayed elevated. Common Sense Media's tween and teen data shows the 2019-to-2021 pandemic-era increase (17% for both age groups) has not reversed in subsequent years, and adult phone-checking frequency has continued climbing based on year-over-year survey data.

**How can I track and reduce my own screen time?**
Start with your phone's built-in screen time report to see actual numbers rather than a guess. From there, set app-specific limits on your highest-usage apps, move distracting apps off your home screen, and consider a faith-based blocker like FaithLock, which places a Bible verse between you and your most-used apps as a built-in pause point.
