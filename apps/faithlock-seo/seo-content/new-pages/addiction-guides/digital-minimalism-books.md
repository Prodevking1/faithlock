## Key Takeaways

- Every title below has been checked against library and publisher records. Author, year, publisher and subtitle are as printed, not as remembered.
- The list is grouped by problem, not by rank. The right first book depends on whether you want a philosophy, a method, the underlying science, or a theology.
- If you only read one, read Cal Newport's *Digital Minimalism*. If you only read one and your problem is specifically the phone in your hand, read Catherine Price instead.
- Reading about attention is a recognized way of avoiding doing anything about it. Pick one, finish it, act on it, then consider a second.

## Start Here: The Philosophy

### 1. Digital Minimalism: Choosing a Focused Life in a Noisy World

**Cal Newport, 2019, Portfolio (Penguin)**

The book that put the phrase into circulation, published February 5, 2019. Newport's proposal, in the publisher's words, is "a minimalist approach to technology use in which you radically reduce the time you spend online, focusing on a small set of carefully-selected activities while happily ignoring the rest."

Its practical core came out of a digital declutter experiment Newport ran in January 2018 that, per his own account, "ended up growing to over 1,600 participants and receiving coverage in the *New York Times*." That is why the method section reads like something observed rather than something theorized.

Best for: you want a framework you can apply to every future tool, not just the current ones. See [digital minimalism](/resources/digital-minimalism) for the summary.

### 2. How to Do Nothing: Resisting the Attention Economy

**Jenny Odell, 2019, Melville House**

The literary counterweight to the productivity framing. Odell is an artist, and her argument is not that you should reclaim your attention in order to get more done. It is that attention has value that has nothing to do with output. Denser and slower than the others here, and the only one on this list that will change how you look at a park.

Best for: the productivity framing feels like it is missing the point, and you suspect it is.

### 3. Deep Work: Rules for Focused Success in a Distracted World

**Cal Newport, 2016, Grand Central Publishing**

Newport's earlier book, aimed at work rather than life. *Digital Minimalism* extends this argument outside the office, so reading them in order is logical but not required.

Best for: the problem is concentration during working hours specifically.

## If the Problem Is Your Phone Specifically

### 4. How to Break Up with Your Phone: The 30-Day Plan to Take Back Your Life

**Catherine Price, 2018, Ten Speed Press**

The most practical book on this list. Half explanation, half a structured 30-day program with something to do each day. If you find Newport too philosophical for your current mood, start here instead.

Edition note: a revised edition (Ten Speed Press, February 2025) carries a different subtitle, "The 30-Day Digital Detox Plan." Both are the same author's work on the same subject.

Best for: you do not want a philosophy, you want instructions. Pair it with [how to do a digital detox](/resources/how-to-do-a-digital-detox).

### 5. Indistractable: How to Control Your Attention and Choose Your Life

**Nir Eyal, 2019, BenBella Books**

Notable because Eyal previously wrote *Hooked*, a book teaching product designers how to build habit-forming technology. *Indistractable* argues distraction begins with internal discomfort rather than with the device, which puts him at odds with the more structural books here. Read it alongside Hari or Alter rather than instead of them.

Best for: you want the argument from someone who helped build the thing.

### 6. Irresistible: The Rise of Addictive Technology and the Business of Keeping Us Hooked

**Adam Alter, 2017, Penguin Press**

The design case. Alter is a marketing and psychology professor, and this is the book explaining why these products are engineered the way they are.

Best for: you want to understand the mechanism before changing the behavior.

## If You Want the Science and the Systems

### 7. Stolen Focus: Why You Can't Pay Attention—and How to Think Deeply Again

**Johann Hari, 2022, Crown**

The most ambitious argument on the list: that the attention crisis is structural rather than personal, driven by factors including sleep deprivation, diet, pollution and work design as well as technology. Hari is a journalist, his previous books have drawn methodological criticism, and this one is best read as a well-reported synthesis rather than as a primary scientific source.

Edition note: the UK edition (Bloomsbury) carries the shorter subtitle "Why You Can't Pay Attention."

Best for: you suspect blaming yourself is the wrong frame.

### 8. Dopamine Nation: Finding Balance in the Age of Indulgence

**Anna Lembke, 2021, Dutton**

Lembke is a Stanford psychiatrist who runs an addiction clinic, and this is the closest thing on the list to a clinical account of compulsive consumption. It is also the book most often cited, and most often misread, by the dopamine detox community.

Worth pairing with [what is a dopamine detox](/resources/dopamine-detox), which covers why the popular version of that idea misdescribes the neuroscience, and [does dopamine detox work](/resources/does-dopamine-detox-work) for the outcome data.

Best for: you want a clinician's view of compulsion rather than a productivity writer's.

### 9. The Anxious Generation: How the Great Rewiring of Childhood Is Causing an Epidemic of Mental Illness

**Jonathan Haidt, 2024, Penguin Press**

About children rather than adults, and the most contested book here. Haidt argues smartphones and social media caused a measurable adolescent mental health decline, and a substantial number of researchers dispute the strength of that causal claim. Read it knowing the debate exists.

Best for: you are a parent. Pair with [teen phone addiction](/resources/teen-phone-addiction), which covers why unilateral control tends to produce concealment rather than change.

### 10. Bored and Brilliant: How Spacing Out Can Unlock Your Most Productive and Creative Self

**Manoush Zomorodi, 2017, St. Martin's Press**

Grew out of a public radio project in which listeners completed a week of challenges to reclaim boredom. Light, and it makes one genuinely useful point: boredom is not the failure state of a good evening, it is the precondition for a lot of thinking.

Best for: you got through the first three days of a detox and the boredom is winning.

## The Christian Shelf

### 11. The Tech-Wise Family: Everyday Steps for Putting Technology in Its Proper Place

**Andy Crouch, 2017, Baker Books**

The most useful Christian book on this list for households with children, because it commits to specific practices rather than principles. Crouch's framing is about what a family is for, with technology decisions following from that.

Best for: you want household rules that come from something other than fear.

### 12. 12 Ways Your Phone Is Changing You

**Tony Reinke, 2017, Crossway**

No subtitle, and a more theological approach than Crouch's. Reinke works through twelve specific effects, and the book is less about rules than about what constant connection does to a person over time.

Best for: you want the theology worked out rather than assumed.

### 13. The Ruthless Elimination of Hurry: How to Stay Emotionally Healthy and Spiritually Alive in the Chaos of the Modern World

**John Mark Comer, 2019, WaterBrook**

Not primarily about phones. It is about speed, of which the phone is one symptom, and it makes the case for sabbath, silence and slowness as spiritual practices rather than as self-care.

Best for: the phone is the presenting problem and pace is the actual one. See [digital minimalism for Christians](/resources/digital-minimalism-christian).

### 14. The Common Rule: Habits of Purpose for an Age of Distraction

**Justin Whitmel Earley, 2019, InterVarsity Press**

Proposes a specific rule of life built from four daily and four weekly habits, several of which are directly about screens. The most concrete of the four Christian titles here.

Best for: you want a structure to actually follow, not a book to agree with.

## The Experiment

### 15. 24/6: The Power of Unplugging One Day a Week

**Tiffany Shlain, 2019, Gallery Books**

Shlain and her family kept a weekly screen-free day for years, and this is the account of what that was actually like, including the parts that did not work. The most useful book here if you want one specific practice rather than a whole philosophy.

Best for: you want to try one thing this week. Pair with [phone-free weekend](/resources/phone-free-weekend).

## One More, With a Caveat

**Hyperfocus, Chris Bailey, first published 2018.** A useful book on attention management, listed separately because its publisher and subtitle vary by market in a way we could not resolve to a single verified US edition. Attested editions include Pan Macmillan (UK, 2018), Random House Canada (2018) and Penguin Books (2019), with three different subtitles across them. If you buy it, check which edition you are getting.

## How to Choose One

The failure mode with this genre is reading five of these books and changing nothing, because reading about attention feels productive and costs nothing.

- **Want a framework?** Newport, *Digital Minimalism*.
- **Want a program starting Monday?** Price, *How to Break Up with Your Phone*.
- **Want to understand why it is not your fault?** Hari or Alter.
- **Parenting?** Crouch, then Haidt.
- **Christian and want practices?** Earley or Comer.
- **Want one book that will bother you productively?** Odell.

Then do one thing from it before buying the next.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the best book on digital minimalism?**
Cal Newport's *Digital Minimalism: Choosing a Focused Life in a Noisy World* (Portfolio, 2019) is the definitive one, because it is where the term became widely used and it supplies a framework rather than only a critique. If you want instructions instead of a philosophy, Catherine Price's *How to Break Up with Your Phone* is the better first purchase.

**Did Cal Newport write more than one book about technology?**
Yes. *Deep Work* (2016) covers concentration at work, *Digital Minimalism* (2019) covers personal technology use, and he has written several other books on work and productivity. The two listed here are the relevant ones for this subject.

**Are there Christian books on digital minimalism?**
Several. Andy Crouch's *The Tech-Wise Family* (Baker Books, 2017) is the most practical for households, Tony Reinke's *12 Ways Your Phone Is Changing You* (Crossway, 2017) the most theological, and John Mark Comer's *The Ruthless Elimination of Hurry* (WaterBrook, 2019) the most widely read, though it treats phones as a symptom of a larger problem with pace.

**Which book should I read if I have kids?**
Andy Crouch's *The Tech-Wise Family* for practices, and Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* for the argument about adolescent mental health, keeping in mind that Haidt's causal claims are actively disputed by other researchers.

**Do I need to read a book to do this at all?**
No. The core method fits on an index card: decide what you value, remove the tools that do not serve it, replace the time with something specific, then reintroduce deliberately with rules. The books help with motivation and with the parts that go wrong, which is a real contribution but not a prerequisite.
