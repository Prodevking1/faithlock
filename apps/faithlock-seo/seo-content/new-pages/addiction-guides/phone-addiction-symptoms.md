## Key Takeaways

- No official symptom list exists, because phone addiction is not a diagnosis in the DSM-5 or ICD-11. What follows comes from research screening tools, not a clinical manual.
- The signs researchers measure cluster into four groups: loss of control, tolerance, withdrawal-like discomfort, and interference with real life.
- Heavy use and problematic use are different things. The dividing line is not hours. It is whether you can stop when you decide to.
- A high score on a screening scale is a reason to pay attention, not a diagnosis.

---

## Why There Is No Official Symptom List

If you searched for phone addiction symptoms expecting a checklist from a medical body, here is the honest situation.

The World Health Organization's ICD-11 recognizes [gaming disorder](https://www.who.int/standards/classifications/frequently-asked-questions/gaming-disorder), with criteria built around impaired control, escalating priority given to gaming, and continuation despite negative consequences, normally sustained for at least 12 months. It did not extend that to phones generally. The DSM-5 does not list phone addiction either.

Some researchers argue the label is wrong entirely. A review in the Journal of Behavioral Addictions examined the literature and concluded there was not sufficient support to confirm smartphone addiction as an addiction, proposing "problematic" or "maladaptive" smartphone use instead ([Panova and Carbonell, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6174603/)).

So the phone addiction symptoms below are not diagnostic criteria. They are the behaviors that show up repeatedly in validated research instruments and in reviews of the field. That is a weaker claim than a diagnosis, and it is still worth reading, because the behaviors are real even when the label is contested.

## The Four Clusters

### 1. Loss of control

- You open an app you did not consciously decide to open
- You go past a limit you set yourself, then set the same limit again
- You say "five minutes" and lose forty
- You have tried to cut back and did not manage it

This is the cluster that matters most. Everything else can be explained away. This one cannot.

### 2. Tolerance

- The amount of scrolling that used to feel like enough does not any more
- You need two screens now, phone plus TV, because one is not stimulating enough
- Content that satisfied you a year ago feels slow

### 3. Discomfort when separated

- Low-grade anxiety when the phone is in another room or at 4% battery
- Reaching for it in a pocket where it is not
- Checking it during conversations, in queues, at red lights, without deciding to

### 4. Interference with the rest of your life

- Sleep. In a study of 686 middle and high school students, sleep quality mediated the link between problematic smartphone use and physical health complaints, which suggests much of the damage runs through the bedroom ([Xie, Dong and Wang, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6174583/))
- Attention. A systematic review of 21 fMRI studies found internet and smartphone addiction associated with impairments in cognitive control, in both reward-processing and executive-function regions ([León Méndez et al., Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38320657/))
- Mood. Across 23 studies, problematic smartphone use was consistently associated with depression at medium or larger effect sizes and with anxiety at smaller ones ([Elhai et al., Journal of Affective Disorders, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27736736/))
- Relationships. The people you live with have started commenting

On that last research point, a caution the authors themselves make: these are correlations. Nobody has established which direction the arrow points. Depression may drive the scrolling as much as the scrolling deepens the depression, and for most people it is probably both.

## A Screening Scale You Can Actually Take

The most widely used short instrument is the Smartphone Addiction Scale Short Version. It has ten items, was validated on 540 Korean adolescents with an average age of 14.5, and reported a Cronbach's alpha of 0.911. The published cut-off scores were 31 for boys and 33 for girls ([Kwon, Kim, Cho and Yang, PLoS One, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3877074/)).

Two honest caveats before you use it. It was validated on teenagers in one country, so applying the exact cut-off to a 45 year old in Ohio is a stretch. And a score above the line means "worth looking into," not "you have a condition."

If you want something simpler, answer these six questions with a plain yes or no:

1. Is your phone the first thing you touch in the morning and the last at night?
2. Have you tried to cut back in the last six months and failed?
3. Do you feel uneasy when you cannot check it?
4. Do you use it longer than you intended most days?
5. Has someone close to you raised it with you?
6. Are you using it to avoid a feeling rather than to do a task?

Three or more yeses puts you in the territory this whole cluster of pages is about. Six means it is worth a conversation with someone qualified. Question 6 alone is the one to take most seriously.

## Heavy Use Versus a Real Problem

The most common mistake in self-assessment is using hours as the test.

Reviews.org surveyed about 1,000 US adults in Q4 2025 and found an average of 186 phone checks a day and 3 hours 14 minutes of use, with 45.8% calling themselves addicted ([Reviews.org, January 2026](https://www.reviews.org/mobile/cell-phone-addiction/)). If average use were the threshold, half the country would qualify, which tells you the threshold is wrong.

| | Heavy use | Problematic use |
|---|---|---|
| Control | You could stop; you just do not want to | You want to stop and cannot |
| Consequences | Time spent, not much else | Sleep, work, or relationships affected |
| After cutting back | Mild boredom | Restlessness, irritability, low mood |
| Function | Entertainment, work, connection | Avoiding a feeling |
| Honest self-report | "I use it a lot" | "I do not want to look at the number" |

A translator who spends six hours a day on a phone for work is a heavy user. Someone who spends ninety minutes a day but cannot make it through dinner without checking, and feels worse afterwards, is the one with the problem.

## When to Stop Self-Assessing and Talk to Someone

Take this to a professional rather than a blog if any of the following is true: repeated genuine attempts to cut back have failed, your job or studies are slipping, you are losing significant sleep, or your phone use is primarily numbing anxiety, grief, or loneliness.

That last one is the important one. If the phone is the medicine, taking the medicine away without treating the wound does not usually go well. [Help for phone addiction](/resources/help-for-phone-addiction) covers where to actually go, and [phone addiction therapy](/resources/phone-addiction-therapy) covers what the evidence says about the treatments on offer.

## A Note for Christians Reading This

There is a version of this self-assessment that turns into self-flagellation, and it makes things worse. Guilt about wasted hours feels like conviction, but it usually functions as fuel: you feel bad, you scroll to stop feeling bad, you feel worse.

Paul's line is more useful than a symptom checklist. "I have the right to do anything, you say, but not everything is beneficial. I have the right to do anything, but I will not be mastered by anything" (1 Corinthians 6:12, NIV). He is not talking about sin. He is talking about permissible things that quietly take charge. That is the question to ask about your phone, and it is a calmer question than "am I addicted."

## If the Distress Goes Deeper Than the Phone

Some of the symptoms above overlap with anxiety and depression, and the phone can be the visible edge of something heavier. If you recognize hopelessness or thoughts of self-harm in yourself, that comes first. In the US, the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/) is available by call, text, or chat, 24/7, free, in English and Spanish.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What are the physical symptoms of phone addiction?**
The commonly reported ones are disrupted sleep, eye strain, and neck and hand pain. The sleep link has the most support: in the 686-student study cited above, sleep quality accounted for much of the relationship between problematic use and physical complaints. Be careful with claims of brain damage. One MRI study did find lower gray matter volume in several regions among people meeting psychometric criteria for smartphone addiction, but it compared 22 people with 26 controls ([Horvath et al., Addictive Behaviors, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32062336/)). That is a small study and it is not proof of anything permanent.

**How many hours a day counts as phone addiction?**
No hour count qualifies on its own. The research instruments ask about control and consequences, not duration, which is why two people with identical screen time can have completely different problems.

**Is phone addiction a mental illness?**
Not as a formal diagnosis. It appears in neither the DSM-5 nor the ICD-11 as a distinct disorder. Some researchers argue it should be, others argue the addiction framing is wrong. What is not disputed is that the behavior pattern causes real problems for some people.

**Can phone addiction symptoms be a sign of something else?**
Frequently, yes. Heavy compulsive use travels with depression and anxiety, and the direction of causation is unresolved in the literature. If your symptoms started alongside a low period, treat the low period as the more important thing.

**What is the difference between phone addiction and social media addiction?**
Mostly scope. Social media is one of several delivery mechanisms on a device that also holds games, news, shopping, and messaging. Some people's problem is one app. Others reach for the device itself regardless of what is on it, and for them removing a single app just relocates the behavior.
