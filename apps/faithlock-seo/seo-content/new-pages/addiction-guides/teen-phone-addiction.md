## Key Takeaways

- Pew found 46% of US teens say they are online almost constantly, roughly double the figure from a decade earlier.
- Teen phone addiction is not a diagnosis. The closest recognized category is gaming disorder in ICD-11, and it has a 12 month criterion for a reason.
- Sleep is where most of the measurable damage shows up, which makes the bedroom the highest-value place to start.
- Surveillance-first approaches tend to produce concealment. The goal worth aiming at is a teenager who can regulate themselves, because in two years nobody will be doing it for them.

---

## What the Numbers Actually Say

Pew Research Center surveyed 1,391 US teens aged 13 to 17 between September 18 and October 10, 2024. Nearly half, 46%, said they are online almost constantly, up from 24% a decade earlier. Ninety-five percent have access to a smartphone ([Pew Research Center, December 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/12/12/teens-social-media-and-technology-2024/)).

That is the reliable number. Here is the one people quote at you that is not established: that this constitutes an addiction in any clinical sense.

The WHO's ICD-11 recognizes [gaming disorder](https://www.who.int/standards/classifications/frequently-asked-questions/gaming-disorder), and its criteria are demanding: impaired control, escalating priority given to gaming over other activities and daily life, continuation despite negative consequences, significant impairment of functioning, and normally at least 12 months of this pattern. Phone use in general was not included. The DSM-5 does not list it either. A review in the Journal of Behavioral Addictions concluded there was not sufficient support to treat smartphone use as an addiction and proposed "problematic use" instead ([Panova and Carbonell, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6174603/)).

Why this matters for a parent: a teenager who is online constantly and doing fine at school, sleeping, and seeing friends is in a different situation from one whose life is visibly narrowing. Treating the first as though they were the second is how you lose the conversation you actually need to have.

## Where the Real Damage Shows Up

If you only have the energy to fight one battle, make it sleep.

In a study of 686 middle and high school students, sleep quality mediated the relationship between problematic smartphone use and physical health symptoms, meaning much of the harm ran through the fact that the phone was wrecking sleep ([Xie, Dong and Wang, Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6174583/)).

Beyond sleep, a review of 38 studies on risk factors for problematic smartphone use in children and adolescents found that using a smartphone for gaming and social networking may be risk factors, alongside social, family, and personality factors ([Fischer-Grote, Kothgassner and Felnhofer, Neuropsychiatrie, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31493233/)). And in the broader literature, problematic smartphone use is consistently associated with depression at medium or larger effect sizes ([Elhai et al., Journal of Affective Disorders, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27736736/)), with the direction of causation unresolved.

Read that last point carefully before acting on it. If your teenager is anxious or depressed and also on their phone constantly, confiscating the phone treats the visible half. It may make the invisible half worse.

## Why Control-First Approaches Backfire

Most advice for parents on teen phone addiction is about tightening control: monitoring software, confiscation, contracts. Some of that has a place. As a primary strategy it has a predictable failure mode.

A teenager subject to unilateral control learns to route around it. The second phone, the friend's device, the school laptop, the app that looks like a calculator. What you gain in short-term compliance you pay for in accurate information about your kid's life, which is a bad trade at 15.

There is a second problem, harder to see. Control that works only while it is enforced teaches nothing that survives it. At 18 they leave with the same impulse regulation they had at 14, minus your passcode. Externally imposed limits and internal self-discipline are not the same skill, and only one of them travels.

None of this means no rules. It means the rules work best when they are household rules rather than punishments, when they apply to adults too, and when the teenager has a hand in setting them.

## What Actually Tends to Work

**Start with the household, not the teenager.** A no-phones-at-dinner rule that the parents also follow is a rule. One that only applies to the kid is a punishment, and it gets treated as one. Charging every phone in the kitchen overnight, adults included, is the single highest-value change available and it removes the argument about whose phone matters.

**Fix sleep before anything else.** Phones out of bedrooms at a set time. Buy alarm clocks. Given the evidence on sleep as the mediating pathway, this earns more than any app limit.

**Ask what they get from it, and listen.** Most teen phone use is social, and telling a 15 year old their friendships are fake goes about as well as you would expect. You want to know whether they are connecting, escaping, or comparing, because the response differs for each.

**Negotiate rather than dictate, where you can.** A teenager who helped set the limit defends it. One who had it imposed treats defeating it as a game. You are not going to win a technical arms race with someone who has more time than you.

**Model it.** This one is unwelcome and it is the most predictive. A parent scrolling through dinner has no available argument.

**Watch for the real warning signs.** Not hours. Withdrawal from activities they used to like, grades sliding over months rather than a bad week, sleep collapsing, secrecy that is new, or distress rather than annoyance when separated from the device. That combination warrants a conversation with your pediatrician or a counselor, not a stricter setting.

## When to Get Outside Help

Bring in a professional if: the changes above have been genuinely tried for a couple of months and nothing moved; school performance or friendships are deteriorating over time; sleep is severely disrupted; or you suspect anxiety, depression, or something they are not telling you.

Ask for someone who works with adolescents and uses CBT. A meta-analysis of 29 papers covering 5,601 children and young adults with internet addiction or internet gaming disorder found that combining pharmacotherapy with CBT or multi-level counseling was the most effective option studied, with medication targeting co-occurring conditions rather than the technology use itself ([Chang et al., IJERPH, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8909504/)).

## A Word to Christian Parents

Two temptations worth naming.

The first is treating this as a purity problem when it is often an attention and loneliness problem. Not every hour on a phone is a spiritual battle, and treating it that way spends your credibility on the wrong fights, leaving you nothing when a real one arrives.

The second is using Scripture as leverage. A verse deployed to win an argument teaches a teenager that the Bible is a tool parents use against them, which is a lesson that outlasts the argument by decades.

What works better is what worked for you: honesty about your own struggle with the same device. "Confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed" (James 5:16, NIV). A parent who says "I check my phone before I pray and I hate it" has a conversation available that no lecture opens.

## If Your Teen Is in Crisis

If the phone use sits alongside thoughts of self-harm, hopelessness, or withdrawal that frightens you, stop working on screen habits and address that first. In the US, the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/) is available by call, text, or chat, 24/7, free, and it takes calls from parents worried about a child, not only from the person in crisis.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I know if my teenager has teen phone addiction?**
Look at trajectory rather than hours. Withdrawal from activities they used to enjoy, grades declining across months, sleep collapsing, new secrecy, and real distress rather than irritation when separated from the phone. Constant use with none of those is normal adolescence in 2026, and Pew figure reflects that.

**Should I take my teenager's phone away?**
As a sanction for a specific broken agreement, sometimes. As a strategy, rarely. It typically produces workarounds and less honest information, and it teaches nothing that survives the moment they get it back. Removing it from the bedroom overnight gets most of the benefit with far less conflict.

**Is monitoring software a good idea?**
For younger children, supervision is appropriate and expected. For older teenagers, covert monitoring is a significant trust cost, and if it is discovered, and it usually is, you tend to pay more than you gained. If you use it, tell them.

**At what age should a child get a smartphone?**
There is no research-backed threshold, and anyone quoting one confidently is overstating the evidence. What the risk-factor literature suggests is that readiness varies by child, and that how the phone is used matters more than when it arrives.

**How much screen time is healthy for teens?**
No credible body publishes an hour figure for teenagers, because the research measures control and consequences rather than duration. Sleep, school, physical activity, and in-person friendship are the practical tests. If those four are intact, the number matters much less than it feels like it should.
