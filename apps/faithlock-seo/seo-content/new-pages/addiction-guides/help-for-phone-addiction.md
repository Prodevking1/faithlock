## Key Takeaways

- Most people looking for help for phone addiction need accountability and better defaults, not treatment. A smaller group needs a therapist, usually because something else is underneath.
- No clinic can treat "phone addiction" as a billable diagnosis, because it is not one. Good clinicians treat what is under it and address the behavior alongside.
- Church community is underrated here, and it is free. It is also not a substitute for a counselor when the situation warrants one.
- Residential programs for compulsive technology use exist, but they are aimed at a narrow group.

---

## First, Which Level Do You Need?

Help for phone addiction comes in four levels, and most advice fails because it hands people level three when they needed level one.

| Level | Situation | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Self-directed | You use the phone too much and know it | Friction, limits, replacements, tracking |
| 2. Accountability | You know what to do and do not do it alone | A partner, small group, shared screen time |
| 3. Professional | Repeated failed attempts, real damage to sleep, work, or relationships | Counselor or therapist, usually CBT-based |
| 4. Intensive | Compulsive use has displaced normal functioning, often alongside gaming or other conditions | Structured outpatient or residential program |

Be honest about which line you are on. Going to level three for a level one problem wastes money. Staying at level one for a level three problem wastes years.

## Level 1: Change the Environment Before You Change Yourself

The most reliable finding in this space is that friction beats resolve. In a field experiment on a pause-before-open intervention, users abandoned 36% of their app-opening attempts, and target app openings dropped 57% over six weeks. The controlled version showed that the delay and the dismiss option carried the effect, while a motivational message alone did nothing ([Grüning, Riedel and Lorenz-Spreen, PNAS, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9974409/)).

Free tools that do this today:

- iOS Screen Time: per-app daily limits, Downtime, and a passcode you can hand to someone else
- Android Digital Wellbeing: App timers that close the app and dim the icon when the limit hits, plus Focus mode and Bedtime mode ([Google Support](https://support.google.com/android/answer/9346420))
- Grayscale, notification removal, and moving apps off the home screen, which cost nothing

The full sequence is laid out in [how to break phone addiction](/resources/how-to-break-phone-addiction). If you have not tried level one properly for three weeks, try it before paying anyone.

## Level 2: Accountability, Including the Church Kind

Compulsive habits shrink when they are visible. This is the cheapest intervention available and the one most people skip because it is uncomfortable.

**What works in practice:**

- One person receives your weekly screen time screenshot. Not to scold you. To see it.
- Someone else holds your Screen Time passcode, which removes the Ignore Limit escape hatch on iOS
- A standing question in a small group: "how was your phone this week," asked of everyone, not just you

For Christians there is an existing structure for this, and it does not need inventing. "Confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed" (James 5:16, NIV). Most small groups will pray about anxiety and marriage and money, and never once ask about the thing everyone in the room spends four hours a day doing. Being the person who raises it usually opens a floodgate.

A caution worth stating plainly: accountability is not surveillance. Handing another adult monitoring access to your device is a different thing from choosing to tell someone the truth, and the second one is what actually changes behavior.

If you want language for that conversation, [a prayer for phone addiction](/resources/prayer-for-phone-addiction) and [Bible verses for phone addiction](/resources/bible-verses-phone-addiction) are both written to be used with someone else rather than alone.

## Level 3: Finding a Professional

Here is the part nobody says out loud. You cannot walk into a clinic and be treated for phone addiction as such, because it is not in the DSM-5 or the ICD-11. ICD-11 recognizes [gaming disorder](https://www.who.int/standards/classifications/frequently-asked-questions/gaming-disorder), and that is the closest formal category. One review argued the addiction framing is wrong for phones altogether and proposed "problematic use" instead ([Panova and Carbonell, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6174603/)).

What a good clinician does instead is treat what is driving it. That matters, because across 23 studies problematic smartphone use was consistently associated with depression at medium or larger effect sizes and with anxiety at smaller ones ([Elhai et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27736736/)). Those studies cannot prove direction, but in practice a large share of heavy compulsive use turns out to be self-medication.

**Where to look:**

- The [Psychology Today therapist directory](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/therapists) lets you filter by location and by concern, including Addiction and Cognitive Behavioral (CBT)
- [Christian Care Connect](https://christiancareconnect.com), the provider directory run by the [American Association of Christian Counselors](https://www.aacc.net/), which describes itself as the world's largest Christian mental health organization, if you want a counselor who shares your framework
- Your insurer's in-network list, which is unglamorous and often the fastest route

**What to ask on the first call:** whether they work with behavioral or compulsive habits, whether they use CBT, and whether they screen for depression and anxiety. If the answer to the last one is no, keep calling.

For what the evidence actually says about these approaches, including their limits, see [phone addiction therapy](/resources/phone-addiction-therapy).

## Level 4: Structured and Residential Programs

These exist and they are real, but they are aimed at a narrow group, typically people whose compulsive technology use has collapsed normal functioning, often centered on gaming.

[reSTART Life](https://netaddictionrecovery.com/) in Washington State describes itself as the first program in the nation offering specialized tracks for digital distractions including gaming, social media, and compulsive internet use. It runs outpatient intensive programs, partial hospitalization, and residential care for teens aged 16 to 18 and for adults, and treats co-occurring anxiety, depression, trauma, ADHD, and autism spectrum conditions.

Note what that description implies. The co-occurring list is not decoration. Programs at this level exist largely because the technology use sits on top of something else.

[Phone addiction rehab](/resources/phone-addiction-rehab) goes into who these programs are genuinely for, and who is better served by outpatient care.

## If You Are in Crisis

If the phone use is tangled up with thoughts of self-harm, hopelessness, or a crisis of any kind, skip everything above. In the US, the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/) is available by call, text, or chat, 24/7/365, in English and Spanish, with free one-on-one support for mental health struggles, emotional distress, and alcohol or drug use concerns.

## Where an App Fits, and Where It Does Not

An app cannot provide accountability or treatment. What it can do is hold the line at the moment of the reach, which is the moment willpower is weakest.

[FaithLock](/) is an iOS app that shields the apps you choose using Apple's Family Controls API. Reopening one requires reading a Bible verse and answering a fill-in-the-blank question about it, drawn from the complete Berean Standard Bible library of 31,000 or more verses. It tracks Scripture and prayer streaks, includes guided audio prayers and written prayer journaling, and offers a 3-day free trial. It is built for self-discipline, not for monitoring another person, and it does not exist on Android.

That is a level one and level two tool. If you are at level three, install what you like, and also make the call.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Where can I get help for phone addiction for free?**
Start with the free tools already on your device, then add one accountability partner. If cost is the barrier to professional help, ask therapists about sliding-scale fees, check whether your employer has an assistance program, and ask your church whether it has counseling referrals or a fund. Many do and almost nobody asks.

**Is there a hotline for phone addiction?**
Not a dedicated one. The [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/) covers mental health struggles and emotional distress generally and is the right number if things are acute, but there is no national phone-addiction line, and any site claiming to be one is worth checking carefully.

**Can a pastor help with phone addiction?**
For accountability, encouragement, and framing, often yes, and pastors are dealing with this in their own lives too. For clinical depression, anxiety, or trauma sitting underneath the behavior, a pastor is not a substitute for a licensed counselor. A good one will say so and refer you.

**Do support groups exist for phone or internet addiction?**
Some twelve-step style groups for internet and technology use exist, and availability varies enormously by location. Meeting quality varies as much as availability, so treat the first meeting as a trial rather than a verdict on the format.

**How do I help someone else with phone addiction?**
Do not confiscate, and do not lecture. Ask what they get out of it, listen to the answer, and offer to be the person who sees their weekly number. Unilateral control moves tend to produce concealment rather than change, particularly with teenagers. See [teen phone addiction](/resources/teen-phone-addiction).
