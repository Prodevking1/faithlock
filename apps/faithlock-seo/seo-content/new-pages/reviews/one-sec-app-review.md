## What Is One Sec?

One sec, made by riedel.wtf apps S.L., is built around a simple mechanic: instead of blocking an app outright, it interrupts the moment you try to open it with a short interstitial, usually a breathing exercise, a reflection prompt, or a phone rotation. The idea, backed by published research on app delays, is that a few seconds of friction is often enough to break the automatic pickup habit.

On iOS, this happens through Shortcuts automation intercepting your app launch. On Android, it relies on the Accessibility Service API instead of a Screen Time style system. There's also an optional blocking mode for scheduled, harder restriction, and a browser extension for websites.

This one sec app review looks at how well that pause actually holds up once the novelty wears off.

## Is One Sec Free?

**One sec has a free tier that fully protects a single app, but covering more than one app, or unlocking every intervention type, requires a paid plan.** A family or lifetime tier is available and reaches up to $149, per the App Store listing accessed August 15, 2026.

If your goal is to interrupt just one specific app (say, Instagram), the free tier genuinely works. Most people who want broader coverage end up needing to pay.

## How It Works

1. **Download** one sec on iOS, Android, or as a browser extension
2. **Choose the app** you want to protect for free, or select multiple apps on a paid plan
3. When you try to open a chosen app, one sec shows an **interstitial**: a breathing exercise, a reflection question, or a prompt to rotate your phone
4. **Complete the interstitial** to proceed into the app
5. Optionally, log **journaling and intention notes** tied to that app opening
6. Turn on **blocking mode** for stricter, scheduled full restriction instead of just an interstitial
7. Use the **browser extension** to add the same friction to websites

The whole interstitial typically takes just a few seconds if you actually engage with it, which is exactly the problem reviewers point to.

## One Sec App Review: Does the Friction Actually Last?

This is the honest crux of any one sec app review. In week one, the pause works. You reach for Instagram, the breathing prompt appears, and it genuinely interrupts the habit loop. By week four, according to a separate review, that same prompt has often become an annoying formality that users tap through without registering it. The mechanism doesn't fail technically, it's simply an interstitial you can dismiss, and dismissible friction tends to lose its bite once it becomes routine.

**Anyone who's tried friction based apps before and stopped noticing them.** If a pause has stopped working for you in the past, one sec's core mechanic may hit the same wall, per the reviews cited above.

## Final Verdict

One sec is a thoughtfully designed app, and the underlying idea (a few seconds of friction can break an automatic habit) is backed by real research. I'd recommend the free tier to anyone who wants a low-commitment first step toward noticing their phone habits.

The honest problem is durability. Reviews consistently describe the same arc: real friction in week one, an ignorable formality by week four. If you need something you can't simply tap through once it becomes routine, one sec's core mechanic isn't built for that job, even with the stricter blocking mode enabled.

This one sec app review's bottom line: try the free tier on your worst app first. If the pause still works for you after a month, great. If you find yourself swiping past it without thinking, that's your sign to look at something with a harder floor, whether that's one sec's own blocking mode or an app like FaithLock that ties the unlock to more than a breath.
