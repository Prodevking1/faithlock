## The Church Already Has a Word for This

Screen time feels like a problem the Church has no vocabulary for, which is why most Catholic writing about it borrows from productivity blogs and adds a verse at the end.

The vocabulary exists. It is temperance, and it has been one of the four cardinal virtues since long before anyone had a phone. The Catechism defines it precisely:

> "Temperance is the moral virtue that moderates the attraction of pleasures and provides balance in the use of created goods. It ensures the will's mastery over instincts and keeps desires within the limits of what is honorable." (CCC 1809)

Read that as a description of your phone and every clause lands. The attraction of pleasures. Balance in the use of created goods. The will's mastery over instincts. A definition written centuries before the infinite scroll describes it better than most things written about it since.

Temperance is listed alongside prudence, justice and fortitude as the virtues on which "all the others are grouped" (CCC 1805). It is not a minor discipline for scrupulous people. It is structural.

## The Verse That Names the Mechanism

All Scripture here is the New American Bible, Revised Edition, the translation approved for use in the United States.

**1 Corinthians 6:12** is the one to memorize:

> "Everything is lawful for me," but not everything is beneficial. "Everything is lawful for me," but I will not let myself be dominated by anything.

Paul is quoting a slogan back at the Corinthians and then qualifying it twice. The second qualification is the useful one. He does not argue that the thing is sinful. He argues that being dominated by it is the problem, whatever it is.

Nothing on your phone is illicit. That is exactly why the ordinary categories fail and why this verse is the right one. The question is not whether opening the app is a sin. The question is who is deciding.

**Sirach 18:30-31**, from the wisdom literature Protestant Bibles do not carry:

> Do not let your passions be your guide, but keep your desires in check. If you allow yourself to satisfy your passions, they will make you the laughingstock of your enemies.

**Galatians 5:22-23** places self-control at the end of the list, not the beginning:

> In contrast, the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control. Against such there is no law.

Fruit, not achievement. The distinction matters when you are three weeks into a resolution and failing. Temperance is a virtue you acquire by repetition and a gift you receive; Catholic teaching holds both, and dropping either one produces a distorted spirituality. Grit alone becomes despair. Waiting alone becomes passivity.

**Psalm 46:11:**

> Be still and know that I am God! I am exalted among the nations, exalted on the earth.

## An Examination of Conscience for the Phone

The daily examination of conscience is standard Catholic practice, and it works on screen time better than any tracking app, because it asks a question the tracking app cannot: not how long, but what happened to you.

St. Ignatius's Daily Examen has five movements. Applied to a phone, they look like this.

**1. Become aware of God's presence.** Before opening the phone's own usage screen, sit for thirty seconds without it. The number is going to provoke something and you want to meet it as a person praying rather than a person defending himself.

**2. Review the day with gratitude.** Include the phone honestly. It carried a message from your mother, a Mass time, a photograph of a godchild. Naming that first prevents the examination from becoming an exercise in self-disgust, which is not what the tradition asks for.

**3. Pay attention to your emotions.** This is the step that does the actual work. What were you feeling in the ninety seconds before you picked it up the last four times? Boredom is the common answer. Anxiety and loneliness are the more honest ones. The phone is rarely the appetite; it is what the appetite reaches for.

**4. Choose one feature of the day and pray from it.** One moment, not the whole pattern. The time you checked something during a conversation with your spouse. Pray about that single moment specifically.

**5. Look toward tomorrow.** One concrete resolution, small enough to keep. Not "less screen time."

Then an Act of Contrition, if there is something to be sorry for. Our [catholic evening prayer](/resources/catholic-evening-prayer) page has the full text and the traditional night prayers this fits inside.

Two guardrails, because this practice gets distorted in both directions. It is not a nightly trial, and if the examination reliably leaves you worse than it found you, raise it with a confessor. And it is not merely a feeling; it ends in a resolution, or it was a mood, not an examen.

## The Actual Rules of Fasting and Abstinence

Most Catholics are vague on these, and the vagueness produces both false guilt and false permission. Here is what the law actually says, and then what it means for screens.

**The universal law**, from the 1983 Code of Canon Law:

| Canon | What it says |
|---|---|
| 1250 | "The penitential days and times in the universal Church are every Friday of the whole year and the season of Lent." |
| 1251 | "Abstinence from meat, or from some other food as determined by the Episcopal Conference, is to be observed on all Fridays, unless a solemnity should fall on a Friday. Abstinence and fasting are to be observed on Ash Wednesday and Good Friday." |
| 1252 | "The law of abstinence binds those who have completed their fourteenth year. The law of fasting binds those who have attained their majority, until the beginning of their sixtieth year." |
| 1253 | The conference of bishops "can determine more precisely the observance of fast and abstinence as well as substitute other forms of penance, especially works of charity and exercises of piety, in whole or in part, for abstinence and fast." |

Majority is completed age eighteen (canon 97 §1), so the law of fasting binds roughly ages eighteen through fifty-nine.

**What a fast is**, from Paul VI's 1966 apostolic constitution *Paenitemini*:

> "The law of fasting allows only one full meal a day, but does not prohibit taking some food in the morning and evening, observing—as far as quantity and quality are concerned—approved local custom."

And abstinence:

> "The law of abstinence forbids the use of meat, but not of eggs, the products of milk or condiments made of animal fat."

In the United States the approved local custom is expressed as one full meal plus two smaller meals that together do not add up to a full meal, with no eating between meals. Days of fast are Ash Wednesday and Good Friday only. Days of abstinence are those two plus every Friday of Lent.

**The part almost nobody knows.** Canon 1250 makes *every* Friday of the year penitential, not just the Fridays of Lent. What the United States bishops did in 1966, using the authority canon 1253 gives them, was allow Catholics to substitute another form of penance for abstinence from meat on Fridays outside Lent. The obligation of Friday penance was never lifted. Only the specific requirement to skip meat was made optional, and only outside Lent.

That is the door. If you are looking for a Catholic frame for screen discipline that is not invented, Friday penance is it, and it is available fifty-two weeks a year rather than six.

## What a Catholic Screen Rule Looks Like

Four practices, in ascending order of difficulty.

**Friday as a screen penance.** Substitute a specific screen abstinence for the Friday meat abstinence outside Lent: no social apps between waking and evening, or no phone at the table, or no news. Choose one and keep it for a season rather than three ambitious ones for a fortnight. It is a real penance in a real category, offered on the day of the Lord's death.

**The first twenty minutes.** Nothing on the screen before the Morning Offering. This is temperance applied at the highest-leverage point in the day, and it costs nothing. The [catholic morning prayer](/resources/catholic-morning-prayer) page has the text.

**The nightly examen.** Five minutes, as above. It is the diagnostic; without it you are treating symptoms you have not identified.

**A Lenten digital fast**, if you want the full form. That is a distinct practice with its own shape and its own rules, covered on our [catholic digital fast](/resources/catholic-digital-fast) page.

What none of these are is a productivity scheme. Tobit 12:8 puts the whole logic plainly: "Prayer with fasting is good." Time reclaimed from a screen and then spent on nothing in particular has been reclaimed for nothing in particular. The point of the empty space is what goes into it.

## When Willpower Is Not the Missing Piece

Temperance is a virtue, which means it is built by repeated acts, which means you need the repeated acts to be possible. If every attempt collapses in the first ten seconds, you are not being formed in anything. Removing the occasion is not a failure of virtue; it is the ordinary prudence the tradition has always recommended alongside it.

Structural fixes first, because they are free and they work. Charge the phone outside the bedroom. Delete the two apps you already know about. Move everything else off the first home screen.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is excessive screen time a sin?**
Scrolling is not itself sinful matter. It becomes a moral question through what the Catechism calls the will's mastery over instincts (CCC 1809), and through what it displaces: prayer you owed, work you owed, people in the room. The useful examination is not "was that a sin" but 1 Corinthians 6:12, "I will not let myself be dominated by anything." Content that is itself gravely wrong is a separate question and belongs in confession.

**What does the Catholic Church say about phone addiction?**
There is no canon on phones. The Church addresses it through temperance, one of the four cardinal virtues (CCC 1805), through the practice of examination of conscience, and through the penitential discipline of Fridays and Lent. Those three together are a more complete framework than most secular screen-time advice, and considerably older.

**Can I give up my phone for Lent instead of food?**
For Fridays outside Lent, yes: the United States bishops permit substituting another penance for abstinence from meat. Inside Lent it is an addition, not a swap. Abstinence from meat on the Fridays of Lent and the fasts of Ash Wednesday and Good Friday are prescribed by canon 1251 and are not yours to trade away. Give up the phone as well, not instead.

**What are the age limits for fasting and abstinence?**
Abstinence binds those who have completed their fourteenth year. Fasting binds those who have reached majority, which is completed age eighteen, until the beginning of their sixtieth year, so through age fifty-nine (canons 1252 and 97 §1). Those outside the ages are still to be taught the meaning of penance, and illness or genuine need excuses the obligation.

**How do I make an examination of conscience about my phone?**
Review the day briefly: what you received, where you failed, what you will do differently. On screens specifically, the productive question is what you were feeling immediately before you reached for it, since boredom, anxiety and loneliness are the usual triggers and the phone is only what they reach for. End with an Act of Contrition and one small resolution.

## The Verdict

Stop treating screen time as a productivity problem with a spiritual garnish. It is a question of temperance, which the Church has taught for a very long time and has practical machinery for: a nightly examen to see the pattern, and Friday penance to do something about it every week rather than once a year in February.

Start with the examen for one week before changing anything. Most people discover that their problem is not the hours, it is the three specific moments, and you cannot see those on a usage chart.
