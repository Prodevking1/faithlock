# Redeeming the Time Ephesians 5:15-16: Making Every Moment Count for God

## Definition

**Redeeming the time** (Ephesians 5:15-16) refers to the biblical imperative to use time strategically and intentionally, particularly in the context of spiritual opposition and distraction. The Greek word "exagorazo" (redeem/buy back) suggests purchasing back time from forces that would waste it, implying active reclamation of hours from idleness, sin, and distraction. In today's context, redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 means deliberately countering digital distractions and mindless consumption to invest hours in what truly matters spiritually.

---

## Understanding Redeeming the Time

### The Original Context of Ephesians 5:15-16

To understand what it means to redeem the time, we must first read Paul's full instruction: "Be very careful, then, how you live—not as unwise but as wise, making the most of every opportunity, because the days are evil" (Ephesians 5:15-16, NIV).

Paul writes this during a period of active spiritual opposition. The Ephesian church faced persecution, false teaching, and pressures to compromise their faith. Against this backdrop, Paul doesn't counsel withdrawal or survival mode. Instead, he calls believers to intentional, strategic living. The phrase "making the most of every opportunity" translates the Greek "exagorazo," which literally means "to buy back" or "to redeem." Imagine someone selling you back time that was stolen—that's the image. In a context where evil forces actively work to waste time and distract believers from God's purposes, Paul calls for redemption: consciously buying back hours from waste and distraction.

The phrase "because the days are evil" is crucial. Paul isn't suggesting that all time is inherently evil or that all earthly activity is worthless. Rather, he's acknowledging that dark spiritual forces are active, seeking to sidetrack believers through temptation, distraction, and despair. Given this reality, strategic, intentional time use becomes a spiritual necessity. Redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 is warfare—the use of time as a spiritual weapon against forces that would steal it.

For modern readers, the relevance is striking. While we might not face Roman persecution, we face sophisticated systems engineered to capture attention. Social media platforms employ psychologists whose job is to maximize your engagement. Gaming apps use variable reward systems derived from slot machine research. Our phones deliver notifications designed to be irresistible. The "evil days" Paul describes have taken on digital form. In such an environment, redeeming the time becomes not a suggestion but a necessity for anyone serious about following Christ.

### Time as Our Most Precious Non-Renewable Resource

In biblical wisdom literature, time is consistently presented as humanity's most precious resource—not money, not possessions, but time. Ecclesiastes 9:10 states, "Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with all your might, for in the realm of the dead, where you are going, there is no work or planning or knowledge or wisdom" (NIV). The psalmist implores, "Teach us to number our days, that we may gain a heart of wisdom" (Psalm 90:12). These passages share a common theme: the scarcity of time should produce wisdom about its use.

The reason time is irreplaceable is simple: you cannot get it back. You can earn more money, acquire new possessions, or make new friends, but you cannot recover a wasted hour. This immutable fact makes redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 not just a nice principle but a existential imperative. Studies on end-of-life regrets consistently show that people regret time wasted on trivial pursuits more than they regret any material losses. A 2023 McKinsey study on digital well-being found that 72% of professionals feel they've wasted significant time on unproductive digital activities—and this regret often intensifies when people recognize the opportunity cost: time that could have been spent on relationships, learning, creative pursuits, or spiritual growth.

This is what makes the modern digital context so insidious. Previous generations could waste time, certainly, but the technology required intentional effort. You had to choose to sit in front of a television. You had to go to a theater or rent a video. Technology required decision points. Modern devices require no such friction—distraction is the default. Your phone is designed to fill every empty moment, every pause, every transition. To redeem the time Ephesians 5:15-16 in this context requires not just good intentions but structural changes and sustained resistance.

### The Cost of Redeemed Time

When Paul uses the language of "redeeming" or "buying back" time, he implies a cost. To purchase back hours from distraction requires giving up something else. If you redeem an hour from social media for prayer, you've made a trade. If you reclaim time from entertainment for service, you've prioritized differently. The beauty and challenge of redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 is that it requires actual sacrifice—not in a penitential, shame-based sense, but in the classical sense of giving something up for something better.

Research on human attention and satisfaction shows this trade is genuinely rewarding. A 2024 Harvard study found that people who spend time in prayer, meditation, or focused relationships report substantially higher life satisfaction than those who spend equivalent time on passive media consumption. Yet the choice requires intention because the passive option is always easier. Your phone doesn't require you to initiate; it simply demands acceptance. Prayer requires starting. A face-to-face conversation requires vulnerability. Deep work requires sustained focus. The path of redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 is the harder road—which is precisely why Paul calls believers to wisdom and careful living.

## Signs You're Wasting Time Instead of Redeeming It

- **Unconscious consumption**: Hours passing before you realize you've been scrolling or gaming
- **Time creep**: Notifications and interruptions fracturing focus, making sustained concentration impossible
- **Regret loop**: Finishing a digital session feeling like you've wasted time, but repeating the behavior tomorrow
- **Invisible time**: Difficulty accounting for hours that have passed—your day disappears into digital consumption
- **Opportunity missed**: Recognizing time spent on screens that could have been invested in relationships, learning, or purpose
- **Decision fatigue**: Too many low-priority digital choices draining willpower from important decisions
- **Sleep disruption**: Late-night screen use stealing sleep hours that are necessary for health and functioning
- **Spiritual stagnation**: Little progress on spiritual disciplines (prayer, Bible study, service) despite good intentions

## Biblical Perspective

### Redemption Language Throughout Scripture

*"Therefore each of you must put off falsehood and speak truthfully to your neighbor, for we are all members of one body. 'In your anger do not sin': Do not let the sun go down while you are still angry, and do not give the devil a foothold."* — Ephesians 4:25-27

Immediately before the instruction to redeem the time, Paul establishes the cosmic stakes. The "foothold" that sin and unforgiveness give the devil includes the ability to steal time—to distract believers from God's purposes and from reconciliation with one another. When you waste hours in bitterness, grudge-holding, or distraction instead of addressing conflict and pursuing peace, you've given the devil a foothold. Redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16, then, isn't just about being more productive. It's about reclaiming time from the devil's destructive work and investing it in God's redemptive purposes.

The language of redemption permeates Scripture because believers are themselves redeemed—purchased back from sin and death at infinite cost. Ephesians 1:7 states, "In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of sins, in accordance with the riches of God's grace" (NIV). Because we have been redeemed, we understand the concept of being bought back. Redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 is an extension of our personal redemption: we apply to our daily time management the same principle that Christ applied to our eternal destiny. Just as Christ paid the ultimate price to buy us back from sin, we buy back hours from distraction to invest in God's kingdom.

### The Wisdom of Ecclesiastes on Time and Eternity

*"There is a time for everything, and a season for every activity under the heavens: a time to be born and a time to die, a time to plant and a time to uproot."* — Ecclesiastes 3:1-2

Ecclesiastes 3 presents one of Scripture's most realistic views of time. The passage acknowledges that life consists of many different seasons and activities, each with its proper moment. There's a time to work and a time to rest, a time to speak and a time to be silent, a time to embrace and a time to refrain from embracing. This wisdom prevents a false asceticism where any moment not spent in prayer is wasted. Redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 doesn't mean every moment must be spiritually intense; it means every moment should be intentional.

Yet Ecclesiastes pairs this celebration of life's rhythms with a penetrating reality: "I have seen the burden God has laid on the human race. He has made everything beautiful in its time. He has also set eternity in the human heart" (Ecclesiastes 3:10-11, NIV). We are created with an eternal perspective—an inner awareness that this life extends beyond the grave. This eternal perspective should shape how we redeem the time Ephesians 5:15-16. A wasted hour of distraction feels small in the moment, but when viewed from the perspective of eternity—when all our earthly time is complete—the opportunity cost becomes clearer. Paul encourages believers to live with this eternal perspective, knowing that how we spend our days shapes who we become and what legacy we leave.

### The Call to Active Wisdom, Not Passive Waiting

*"See to it that no one takes you captive through hollow and deceptive philosophy, which depends on human tradition and the elemental spiritual forces of this world rather than on Christ."* — Colossians 2:8

Paul's instruction to redeem the time Ephesians 5:15-16 is active, not passive. He doesn't say, "Wait for circumstances to improve" or "Hope the devil gives you a break." He says, "Be very careful, then, how you live—not as unwise but as wise." This is an action. Redeeming the time requires wisdom, which in biblical terms means choosing rightly despite pressure and temptation. It means recognizing that forces actively work against your time stewardship—including the "hollow and deceptive philosophies" embedded in technological systems designed to maximize engagement at the expense of your wellbeing.

Wisdom about time means distinguishing between the good, the better, and the best. A particular digital activity might be good or innocent—social media connecting you with friends, gaming providing relaxation, entertainment offering escape. The question redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 poses is not whether an activity is morally wrong but whether it's the best use of your irreplaceable time given your values and God's calling on your life. This kind of wise discernment is harder than rule-keeping, but it's deeper and more transformative.

## Practical Solutions for Redeeming the Time

### Create a Personal Time Audit and Redemption Plan

For one week, track your screen time and discretionary hours with radical honesty. How many hours on social media? Entertainment? Gaming? Communication? Work? Sleep? Use the data not to shame yourself but to see clearly. Then answer: Of these hours, which align with my deepest values? Which represent redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16, and which represent wasting it? Based on this honest assessment, create a specific redemption plan: Which hours will you reclaim? What will you redirect them toward—prayer, relationships, learning, service?

### Establish Time Boundaries as Spiritual Discipline

Redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 requires structural support, not just willpower. Implement specific boundaries: "No screens after 9 PM," "Social media only 30 minutes daily during designated time," "One device-free evening weekly." These aren't punishments but structures that protect your ability to be wise. Just as military organizations maintain discipline through structure, and athletic teams thrive through consistent practice, your capacity to redeem time increases through consistent boundaries that support your deeper commitments.

### Replace, Don't Just Restrict

When you reclaim hours from digital distraction, redirect them toward activities that feed your soul. If you stop gaming, start a hobby. If you eliminate social media, schedule meaningful conversations. If you cut streaming, read books or spend time in nature. The human mind abhors a vacuum; simple restriction creates restless cravings. But replacing a bad habit with a good one satisfies the underlying need—for relaxation, connection, or stimulation—in a more nourishing way. This is what genuine redemption looks like: not deprivation but reorientation toward what truly satisfies.

### Practice the Sabbath Principle Weekly

God modeled redeeming the time by establishing a weekly rhythm of rest. One day per week, cease from labor and striving. Create a digital Sabbath—a 24-hour period completely free from phones, computers, and screens. Use this time for what Paul calls "redeeming": investing in God through prayer and worship, in relationships through presence with loved ones, in rest that restores your soul. A weekly Sabbath reminds you that you're not measured by productivity, that life has rhythms beyond efficiency, and that true security comes from God's provision, not your constant striving.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What counts as "redeeming the time" versus wasting it?

This varies by person and season. Rest and recreation aren't wasted time—they're necessary. The question is whether your discretionary time allocation reflects your values. If you claim family is your priority but spend 5 hours daily on social media and 1 hour with family, there's misalignment. Honest time auditing reveals the answer. Redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 means bringing your actual schedule in alignment with your stated values and God's calling on your life.

### Is work time redeemed or wasted?

Work is inherently valuable and can absolutely represent redeeming the time. Whether you're employed or self-employed, whether your work serves others directly (teaching, ministry, healthcare) or indirectly (creating useful products, maintaining infrastructure), work is how humans participate in God's creative activity. The concern is when work consumes all discretionary time, preventing prayer, relationships, rest, and service. Redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 includes ensuring that work gets its proper place without crowding out equally important dimensions of life.

### How can I redeem time if my schedule is already full?

Full schedules usually reflect poor prioritization more than genuine lack of time. Most people could find 5-10 hours weekly by eliminating mindless digital consumption. The question isn't whether time exists but whether you'll be intentional about protecting space for redemption. This might require saying no to good opportunities to protect space for better ones. It might mean delegating or simplifying in some areas to invest in others. Redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 always involves difficult choices—that's the point. It requires genuine commitment, not just wishful thinking.

### What about entertainment and relaxation—are these wasted time?

Not at all. Rest and recreation are biblical goods. God modeled rest by establishing the Sabbath. Jesus took breaks to withdraw and pray. Entertainment and relaxation restore your soul for the work ahead. The question is proportion and intention. An hour of entertainment daily might be healthy; 6 hours is probably distraction. A movie with your family might be meaningful connection; endless scrolling alone is different. Redeeming the time Ephesians 5:15-16 doesn't eliminate enjoyment; it ensures your leisure is genuinely restorative rather than compulsive escape.
