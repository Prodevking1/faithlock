# Technology and Discipleship: Using Digital Tools for Spiritual Growth

## Definition

**Technology and discipleship** refers to the relationship between digital tools and the spiritual development of believers. It encompasses how technology can either accelerate spiritual growth through Bible study apps, online communities, and educational resources, or hinder discipleship through addictive platforms, digital distractions, and unhealthy comparisons. Discipleship in the technological age requires intentionality about which tools serve our spiritual formation and which distract from it.

---

## Understanding Technology and Discipleship

### How Technology Can Enhance Spiritual Formation

Throughout history, Christians have adopted new technologies to support discipleship. The printing press enabled mass distribution of Bibles, sparking the Reformation. Radio and television carried sermons into millions of homes. Today's digital tools offer unprecedented access to spiritual resources.

A Bible reading app allows you to study Scripture anywhere, anytime. A daily devotional arrives in your inbox. You can listen to teaching from world-class Bible teachers, access sermons from your church when life prevents attendance, and join prayer groups with believers across the globe. A 2024 Lifeway Research study found that 68% of Christians use digital tools for spiritual growth, with Bible apps, podcasts, and online Bible studies being the most common. Many report that technology has deepened their faith by making Scripture study more accessible, interactive, and community-driven.

Technology and discipleship can work together beautifully. Some of the most effective modern discipleship uses digital tools strategically. A small group can use video conferencing to include a member deployed overseas. A new believer can access foundational Bible studies on-demand. A pastor can reach thousands with sermon content. A mentor can share resources and encouragement via messaging. These applications of technology reflect the same impulse that drove the apostles to find new ways to share the gospel—using the tools of their time to deepen faith formation.

The key is intentionality. Technology doesn't automatically serve discipleship; rather, we must choose technology and discipleship practices that align with Scripture's priorities: knowing God, growing in Christ-likeness, and building God's kingdom through community and mission.

### How Technology Can Hinder Spiritual Development

Despite its potential, technology poses significant challenges to modern discipleship. The most insidious is distraction. Neuroscientist Gloria Mark's research at UC Irvine found that the average person is interrupted or switches tasks every 3 minutes. When you're reading Scripture on a tablet, a notification pulls you away. When you're listening to a sermon on your phone, a text arrives. The fragmentation of attention undermines the deep focus that biblical discipleship requires.

Beyond distraction, certain technologies actively work against spiritual formation. Social media platforms are designed to trigger comparison, envy, and validation-seeking—all antithetical to humility and contentment. Gaming and entertainment apps exploit psychological vulnerabilities to encourage addictive use, leaving less time and mental energy for prayer and Scripture study. Pornography, accessible and normalized online, represents a specific discipleship challenge that churches are increasingly recognizing and addressing.

A 2023 American Bible Society study revealed that while 83% of Christians own a Bible app, only 37% actually use it regularly. Yet 62% report daily social media use. This pattern suggests that technology and discipleship exist in tension: we have unprecedented access to spiritual tools, yet digital distractions consume the time and attention that would be devoted to using them. For many Christians, the same device that contains a Bible app, study resources, and prayer reminders is engineered to pull attention toward less spiritually nourishing content.

The challenge of technology and discipleship, then, isn't about whether digital tools are good or bad—it's about creating a digital environment that supports rather than sabotages spiritual growth.

### Biblical Discipleship Standards in the Digital Age

Discipleship has always been about transformation, not information. When Jesus called the disciples, He didn't hand them books or point them to teachers. He said, "Follow me," inviting them into proximity, relationship, and shared experience. Throughout the Gospels, discipleship involves watching Jesus, asking questions, failing, being corrected, and gradually becoming more like Him through intimate relationship.

This model of discipleship—relational, embodied, transformative—doesn't change in the digital age. What changes is the landscape in which it happens. Digital tools can support discipleship when they facilitate these core elements: access to Scripture, connection with other believers, teaching that transforms thinking, and opportunity to practice faith. A video Bible study that sparks authentic conversation within a small group serves discipleship. A sermon podcast that teaches the Word and convicts the heart supports growth. A Bible app that helps you establish a consistent prayer habit enables transformation.

But technology and discipleship can work against each other when digital tools replace rather than support relational discipleship. Watching a sermon online without engaging a local church community limits discipleship. Reading Bible study notes without wrestling with Scripture's implications reduces transformative potential. Engaging in Christian online communities without face-to-face accountability creates the illusion of growth without the deeper formation that comes through embodied relationships.

## Signs of Unhealthy Technology and Discipleship Balance

- **Replacing presence with content**: Consuming Christian content online instead of engaging a local church body
- **Performative faith**: Sharing spiritual updates on social media for validation rather than genuine community
- **Scattered attention**: Using Bible study apps but never reading longer passages due to notification interruptions
- **Comparison and discouragement**: Following Christian influencers and feeling inadequate about your own faith journey
- **Passive consumption**: Watching spiritual content without applying it to your life, prayer, or relationships
- **Digital avoidance of authentic struggle**: Scrolling inspirational quotes instead of facing real spiritual challenges with mentors
- **Distracted prayer**: Phone nearby during prayer time, attention divided between God and notifications
- **Substitution of accountability**: Believing online prayer requests and comments replace face-to-face discipleship relationships

## Biblical Perspective

### The Priority of Relational Discipleship

*"Let no one look only to his own interests, but rather to the interests of others. Let each of you look not only to your own interests, but also to the interests of others."* — Philippians 2:4

When Paul describes the heart of discipleship, he emphasizes mutual care and presence. This value fundamentally shapes how technology and discipleship should relate. Digital tools are valid when they enhance our capacity to know and care for one another. A video call to a housebound believer, a text encouraging a friend in struggle, a recorded sermon for someone who missed service—these serve the core discipleship mandate of loving one another. But tools that isolate us, create false connection, or substitute for genuine relationship undermine Paul's vision.

The principle of Philippians 2:4 is that discipleship isn't primarily about personal spiritual advancement but about mutual formation in community. Technology and discipleship work together when they strengthen our ability to look beyond ourselves to the needs and growth of others. They work against each other when technology enables self-focused consumption of spiritual content without reciprocal commitment to others' growth.

### Discipleship Through Tested, Embodied Faith

*"Not only so, but we also glory in our sufferings, because we know that suffering produces perseverance; perseverance, character; and character, hope."* — Romans 5:3-4

One of the striking differences between online and embodied discipleship is the role of struggle and suffering. In person, a disciple shares both victories and defeats with a mentor. A small group knows your real struggles, not your curated spiritual victories. This vulnerability is where genuine transformation occurs. Romans describes how suffering refines faith into character.

Technology and discipleship become problematic when we use digital platforms to project a polished spiritual image while keeping our real struggles hidden. A Christian can curate an inspiring social media presence while privately battling lust, anxiety, or doubt. The discipleship relationship becomes performative rather than transformative. In contrast, embodied discipleship—meeting with a mentor, joining a small group, serving in a local church—creates unavoidable opportunities for others to see your actual struggle. This isn't pleasant, but it's where Romans 5's promise of character development actually occurs.

This doesn't mean avoiding online Christian community—it means ensuring that online connection supplements rather than replaces the vulnerability and accountability of embodied relationships. When technology and discipleship are rightly balanced, digital tools extend the reach of your discipleship community while face-to-face relationships remain the primary venue for transformation.

### Wise Use of Tools for Kingdom Purposes

*"See to it that no one takes you captive through hollow and deceptive philosophy, which depends on human tradition and the elemental spiritual forces of this world rather than on Christ."* — Colossians 2:8

Paul's warning in Colossians 2:8 extends beyond ancient philosophy to any worldview or system that competes with Christ as the center of our thinking and allegiance. Today's digital technologies often embed such competing worldviews. Social media platforms, built on behavioral psychology and designed to maximize engagement (and advertising revenue), operate from values fundamentally at odds with Christian discipleship: they encourage comparison, vanity, quick judgments, and endless consumption rather than reflection, humility, growth, and discernment.

The relationship between technology and discipleship must involve careful discernment. Not all digital tools are neutral. Some are actively hostile to spiritual formation. A Christian shouldn't naively adopt technology without asking: What values does this tool encode? What habits will it cultivate? What vision of human flourishing does it promote? When these questions reveal misalignment with Christian discipleship, the answer isn't shame but wisdom—the wisdom to recognize that technology and discipleship require intentional curation. ## Practical Solutions for Balancing Technology and Discipleship

### Audit Your Digital Spiritual Diet

List all digital tools you use for spiritual growth: Bible apps, podcasts, YouTube channels, books, online courses, streaming services, social media. For each, ask: Does this tool facilitate deeper knowledge of Scripture? Does it challenge me to grow in Christ-likeness? Does it strengthen my connection to a faith community? Does it encourage obedience and transformation, not just information? Be honest about which tools are assets to technology and discipleship balance and which are distractions wearing spiritual clothing.

### Create a "Discipleship Tech Stack"

Intentionally choose a limited set of digital tools that serve your specific spiritual needs. If you need daily Scripture engagement, choose one Bible app and use it consistently. If you want to hear teaching, subscribe to one or two podcasts. If you're in a small group, use one communication platform. This purposeful selection—rather than passively consuming whatever spiritual content appears in your feed—ensures technology and discipleship work together rather than scattering your attention.

### Protect Embodied Discipleship as Primary

Commit to a local church and a consistent small group or mentoring relationship. These face-to-face connections should be your primary discipleship avenue, with digital tools serving to enhance them. When life gets busy and you must choose between online Bible study and attending your small group, choose the small group. When you must decide between watching a sermon online and worshiping with your church community, choose community. This priority protects what is most irreplaceable: the presence and accountability of believers who know you.

### Implement "Fasting" Periods from Addictive Platforms

If social media, gaming, or entertainment apps compete with your discipleship time, implement regular digital fasts. You might eliminate social media for a month, or skip streaming services during Lent, or declare one day weekly as a "digital Sabbath." These breaks provide several benefits: they demonstrate to yourself that you're not controlled by these platforms, they create space for other spiritual disciplines, and they often clarify which digital habits were truly serving you and which were just filling time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it wrong to use digital tools for Bible study instead of physical Bibles?

Not at all. Throughout history, Christians have used the best available technology to access Scripture: manuscripts, printed Bibles, translations in vernacular languages. Digital Bibles continue this tradition. The question isn't physical versus digital but engaged versus passive. Whether you're studying from a leather-bound Bible or an app, what matters is whether you're wrestling with the text, meditating on it, and allowing it to transform your thinking. Some people find digital tools helpful for cross-referencing, note-taking, and accessibility.

### How much online Christian content is "enough" for discipleship?

Content consumption alone is never sufficient for discipleship. You can watch 50 hours of Bible teaching and experience zero spiritual growth if you're not applying it. Quality matters far more than quantity. One excellent Bible study engaged with a group, discussed thoroughly, and applied to life, produces more growth than 10 hours of passive podcast listening. Aim for depth over consumption. Choose 2-3 excellent resources, engage them deeply, and process them relationally.

### Is social media bad for discipleship?

Social media itself is neutral, but its architecture and incentives create challenges for spiritual formation. The platform is designed to encourage comparison, validation-seeking, and quick judgments—all contrary to Christian discipleship values. However, some Christians use social media intentionally to encourage others, share testimonies authentically, or maintain relationships. The key is intentionality and boundaries. If social media is pushing you toward vanity or comparison, it's hindering your discipleship. If it's genuinely strengthening your community, use it purposefully with clear limits.

### What if I don't have access to a local church or small group?

Digital community can supplement but shouldn't entirely replace embodied community. If your circumstances limit access to a physical church, actively work to find one (online churches now offer in-person gatherings in many cities). In the interim, engage deeply with an online small group, connect with a mentor, and ensure some form of real-time, relational accountability exists. The goal is embodied discipleship; digital tools help bridge gaps while you work toward that full participation.
